Yesterday you saw a model call a single tool — looking up the weather, running a
calculation. Today is about what happens when a real business has many tools: a CRM, a
database, internal documents, a calendar, other software. Connecting a model to all of them
used to mean building a separate, custom connection for every single pairing. Today you'll
learn about the standards emerging to fix that.

## The problem: every connection was custom-built

Imagine every appliance in your house needed its own uniquely shaped wall socket. Your
kettle needs one plug shape, your lamp needs a different one, your phone charger needs a
third. Every time you buy a new appliance, an electrician has to install a new kind of
socket for it. That's expensive, slow, and doesn't scale.

That was the situation with connecting AI models to business systems. If you wanted Model A
to use your CRM, someone had to build that specific connection. If you then wanted Model B
to also use the CRM, that was a second, separate piece of work — even though the CRM hadn't
changed. Multiply that across several models and several tools, and you get a tangle of
one-off, brittle connections that someone has to maintain forever.

## MCP: a common plug shape for connecting AI to your tools

**[MCP (Model Context Protocol)](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/introduction)** is a
standard, common way for an AI model to connect to outside tools and data sources. Instead
of custom-building a connection for every model-and-tool combination, a tool built to the
MCP standard can be plugged into any MCP-compatible model, and any MCP-compatible model can
use any MCP-compatible tool.

It's the same idea as a universal power socket. Once everyone agrees on the same plug
shape, any appliance works with any compatible outlet, instead of every appliance needing
its own custom wiring. MCP is that agreed shape, but for AI models and the systems they need
to reach.

> **Why this matters to you:** this is what turns "we built a custom integration for our
> chatbot" into "we support a standard, so new AI tools plug in with far less custom work."
> That difference shows up directly in your integration budget and in how fast you can adopt
> — or switch — AI vendors.

## A2A: how two different AI agents talk to each other

MCP connects a model to a tool or data source. **[A2A (Agent-to-Agent)](https://a2a-protocol.org/latest/)**
is a related but different standard, for a different problem: letting different AI agents
(Day 2 and Day 14's topic — AI systems that can carry out multi-step tasks on their own,
possibly built by different companies) communicate and coordinate with each other directly.

A simple way to keep the two straight: **MCP is how one AI connects to your company's tools
and data. A2A is how two different AI agents talk to and coordinate with each other.** With
A2A, one agent can hand off part of a task to another agent — the way one employee might ask
a colleague in a different department to handle a piece of a project, rather than trying to
do everything themselves.

**The trap to avoid:** don't assume every "agent" a vendor sells actually uses A2A to talk
to other agents. Plenty of products marketed as multi-agent systems are really one system
calling its own internal functions, dressed up in agent language. Ask specifically whether
separate agents — potentially from different vendors — are coordinating through a standard,
or whether it's one system talking to itself.

## Why standards like this matter to a business leader

The technical detail of how MCP or A2A works isn't the point for you. The point is what a
standard does to your negotiating position and your costs:

- **Lower integration cost.** A tool or model that supports a common standard plugs in with
  far less custom engineering than a one-off integration.
- **Less vendor lock-in.** If your systems speak a common standard, switching AI vendors
  later doesn't mean rebuilding every connection from scratch.
- **A genuine question to evaluate vendors by.** Whether a vendor supports these standards
  is a real signal of whether they're building an open, interoperable product or trying to
  keep you inside a closed system — a fair thing to ask about in any AI vendor evaluation.

**Be honest about where this stands.** MCP and A2A are newer, fast-moving standards. The
exact details will keep evolving, and other standards may emerge or compete with them over
time. What's stable is the underlying problem: connecting AI to the many systems a real
business runs is expensive and slow without some shared standard, and that problem is worth
understanding regardless of which specific standard eventually wins out.

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. A vendor demo shows their AI assistant pulling live data from three different internal
   systems. Ask: *"Did your team build a custom connection to each of those systems, or does
   this use a common standard like MCP?"* — tells you whether adding a fourth system next
   year is a quick plug-in or another expensive build.
2. In a vendor evaluation meeting, someone asks what makes one AI platform more "open" than
   another. Ask: *"Does this platform support MCP, or does connecting our tools mean custom
   integration work every time?"* — turns a vague word like "open" into a concrete,
   checkable feature.
3. A team proposes a "multi-agent system" where several AI agents supposedly coordinate on a
   task. Ask: *"Are these actually separate agents communicating through something like A2A,
   or is this one system calling its own internal functions?"* — separates a real
   multi-agent design from a single system marketed with agent language.
4. During a budget discussion about switching AI vendors next year, someone worries about
   the cost of rebuilding every integration. Ask: *"How much of our current setup relies on
   a common standard versus custom one-off connections?"* — surfaces how locked in you
   actually are, before you're stuck with the answer.

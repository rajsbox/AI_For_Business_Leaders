Today isn't a new topic. It's assembly. Almost every day of this course has handed you one
piece of a real AI strategy, one at a time, in isolation. Today, those pieces get put
together into one thing: a document a real leader could actually write, hand to their team,
and use to make decisions. Tomorrow's capstone asks you to draft a one-page version of it for
your own business. Today is about knowing exactly what goes into each of the four sections
first, and where each piece already came from.

## Why most companies never write this down

Most companies aren't short on AI activity. There's a team quietly using chatbots day to day
(Day 7), a few individual pilots someone started on their own initiative (Day 21), maybe one
official project in flight. What's usually missing isn't effort — it's a written statement of
why any of it is happening, which of it deserves more investment, who's actually in charge of
it, and how much risk the company is willing to carry while doing it.

Without that, AI activity stays scattered: busy, sometimes even useful, but not compounding
into anything bigger than the sum of its parts. Writing down even a rough version of a real
strategy — imperfect, one page, done today rather than never — turns "we're doing AI stuff"
into an actual plan someone can execute, evaluate, and improve.

A real AI strategy has four components. Each one draws directly on days you've already
covered.

## Vision: why you're doing this at all

A vision is a short, specific statement of why your business is investing in AI, and what
it's meant to achieve. The failure mode here isn't having no vision — it's having a vague
one. "We're embracing AI" or "we want to be AI-forward" commits to nothing and rules out
nothing. It won't help anyone decide which project to fund next month.

A real vision picks a lane. Is AI mainly about cutting cost in existing work? Improving the
customer experience? Offering a new capability you genuinely couldn't offer before? Keeping
pace with competitors who are already moving? These aren't mutually exclusive over the long
run, but a strategy has to lead with one of them, not list all four and call it a vision.

The test of a good vision isn't whether it inspires people. It's whether it helps you say no.
A specific vision lets you turn down a flashy but off-target project without a fight, the same
way Day 22 taught you to turn down an exciting-sounding use case that fails the volume and
clarity test. A vague vision can't do that — everything sounds like it fits, so nothing gets
filtered out, and you end up with a scattered pile of unrelated pilots instead of a coherent
push in one direction.

> **Why this matters to you:** saying no to the wrong project is just as valuable as saying
> yes to the right one. A vision that can't reject anything isn't a vision — it's a slogan.

## Use-case portfolio: where Days 22 and 23 stop being theory

A strategy doesn't fund one AI project. It funds a small, deliberately chosen set of them —
a portfolio. This is the section where two entire earlier lessons stop being abstract
frameworks and become an actual list with names on it.

Day 22 gave you the filter for picking candidates in the first place: high-volume, so the
payoff is real and there's enough usage data to measure it, and well-defined, so the task can
actually be built and evaluated. Every item in your portfolio should be able to pass that
test, or you're already repeating the mistake Day 22 warned about.

Day 23 gave you the second decision for each candidate: how to actually get it — buy a
general platform, buy a vertical vendor, partner, or build custom. A portfolio isn't complete
until each use case has both a "what" and a "how sourced" next to it.

A good portfolio usually mixes two kinds of bets. Something safe and quick, to prove real
value early and build confidence before asking for a bigger budget. And something more
ambitious, that actually matches the vision you just wrote rather than just being the easiest
thing available. A portfolio that's all safe bets never earns the vision. A portfolio that's
all ambitious bets rarely survives long enough to prove itself.

**One more thing worth naming as you build this list: your candidates will likely cluster by
business function.** This course deliberately stayed function-agnostic throughout, teaching
the general patterns rather than "AI for sales" or "AI for legal" separately, because the
underlying techniques — RAG, evals, guardrails, tokenomics — apply the same way regardless of
which team is using them. In practice, though, a real portfolio often reveals which function
has the clearest high-volume, well-defined candidates first, and it's worth naming that
pattern honestly in your plan rather than forcing an even spread across every department for
its own sake.

## Operating model: who does what, and how decisions get made

The operating model is the "who does what" layer underneath the portfolio — how AI work
actually gets decided, built, and maintained inside the organization on an ongoing basis, not
just at launch. Without this layer, even genuinely good individual projects from Days 22 and
23 stay one-off efforts. They don't compound into real organizational capability, because
nobody owns making the next one easier than the last.

A real operating model answers a short list of concrete questions:

- **Who approves a new AI project?** Someone has to own saying yes or no, using the vision as
  the filter — not whoever asks loudest.
- **Who's responsible for the data readiness behind each use case?** Day 24 covered why data
  quality sets the ceiling on what any AI system can achieve. That ceiling needs a named
  owner, not a hope that the data stays clean on its own.
- **Who owns observability and cost once something is live?** Days 18 and 19 covered what
  happens after a system ships — someone watching whether it's still working, and someone
  accountable for what it's actually costing to run.
- **Who handles the people side of the change?** New AI tools change how people actually do
  their jobs. Someone has to own reskilling, communication, and the workforce and
  change-management work this week's earlier lessons covered.

None of these need an elaborate org chart. They need a name next to each question. A strategy
that skips this section usually looks fine on the page and falls apart within a few months of
real use.

## Risk posture: one coherent stance, not three separate topics

A risk posture is a deliberate, stated position on how much AI risk your organization is
willing to accept, and under what conditions. Without one, risk decisions get made ad hoc,
project by project, usually by whoever is in the room that week — which means similar risks
get treated inconsistently across the business.

This section pulls together three topics this course covered separately, as if they were
unrelated technical concerns, into one coherent leadership position:

**Reliability.** Day 16 covered why models sometimes state confident, plausible-sounding
answers that are simply wrong — [hallucinations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallucination_(artificial_intelligence)) —
and how grounding reduces that risk. A risk posture states plainly how much of that residual
risk is acceptable for a given use case, and what stakes level requires a human checking the
output before it goes anywhere near a customer or a decision.

**Security.** Day 17 covered how AI systems create new attack surfaces — prompt injection,
data leakage through a connected tool — beyond traditional software risk. A risk posture
states what access an AI system is allowed to have, and what has to be reviewed before it's
granted.

**Governance.** Day 25 covered human-in-the-loop design and the broader governance question of
who's watching what an AI system does. A risk posture states, in plain terms, which categories
of decision always require a human in the loop, what the minimum bar is before something ships
to production — the evals from Day 15 and the pilot-to-production gap from Day 20 both feed
directly into that bar — and who has to sign off before a higher-risk AI use case goes live.

A risk posture doesn't need to be conservative or aggressive in the abstract. It needs to be
*stated*, so that a project's actual risk gets checked against something real instead of
against whoever happens to be reviewing it that day.

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. Your team is reviewing a stack of individual AI pilots that grew organically over the past
   year, and someone calls it "our AI strategy." Ask yourself: *"If I wrote down our vision in
   one sentence, would it actually explain why we built each of these — or would half of them
   not fit it at all?"* — the honest answer tells you whether you have a strategy or just a
   collection of activity.
2. You're about to add a new project to next quarter's roadmap because a competitor just
   announced something similar. Ask yourself: *"Does this pass the Day 22 test — high-volume
   and well-defined — and have I actually decided how we'd source it, or am I skipping
   straight to 'yes' because it sounds urgent?"* — catches a portfolio decision being made on
   competitive anxiety instead of the framework you already have.
3. A new AI tool is being proposed, and everyone in the room is focused on whether it will
   work. Ask yourself: *"If this launches successfully, who is actually responsible for its
   data staying current, its cost staying sane, and its output being monitored six months from
   now?"* — surfaces whether there's a real operating model behind the project, or just
   enthusiasm for getting it live.
4. You're reviewing a higher-stakes AI use case — one that touches customers directly or
   feeds into a real decision. Ask yourself: *"Does this require human review by default under
   our risk posture, and has anyone actually said so out loud, or are we assuming someone
   would catch a problem if one came up?"* — turns an assumed safety net into a real, stated
   rule before something goes wrong.

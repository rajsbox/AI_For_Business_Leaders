There isn't one AI model — there are dozens of serious options, from a handful of large
labs and a growing list of alternatives you can run yourself. Today is about telling those
options apart, and knowing what actually matters when you're picking between them.

## Frontier models vs open-weight models

A **frontier model** is one of the most capable models available at any given time, built
by one of a small number of well-funded labs — Anthropic (Claude), OpenAI (GPT), and Google
(Gemini) are the names you'll hear most. These models are **closed-weight**: the company
runs the model on its own servers, and you access it through an app or an API. You never
get a copy of the model itself.

An **[open-weight model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-weight_model)** is one where the
company publishes the model's internal settings — its weights, covered on Day 1 — for anyone
to download and run. Llama (Meta), Mistral, and DeepSeek are the names you'll hear most here.

A simple comparison: a closed frontier model is like ordering from a professionally-run
restaurant kitchen. You get a great meal, made by experts, and you never see the kitchen —
you just pay per order. An open-weight model is like being handed the recipe book itself.
You can cook it in your own kitchen, change the ingredients, and never pay per meal again —
but now you're the one running the kitchen.

Neither one is simply "better." They're a different trade of convenience for control.

## Why a business would pick open-weight

Three reasons come up again and again. **Cost at scale** — once you're running an
open-weight model on your own infrastructure, you're paying a fixed infrastructure cost
instead of a per-use fee that climbs with every additional message. **Data privacy and
control** — the data never leaves your own servers, which matters for regulated industries
or genuinely sensitive information. **Customization** — you can retrain or adjust an
open-weight model on your own data in ways a closed model doesn't allow, because you have
the actual model, not just access to it.

## Why a business would pick a frontier model anyway

The reasons run the other way just as strongly. **Top-tier capability** — the most capable
models at any given time are usually the closed frontier ones; the best open-weight models
tend to trail a step behind the best closed ones, though the gap narrows over time.
**Nothing to manage** — no servers to provision, no model to keep updated, no specialist
team to hire. **Someone else owns reliability and safety** — uptime, guardrails against
harmful outputs, and security patching are the vendor's job, not yours.

> **Why this matters to you:** this is a genuine trade-off, not a maturity ladder where
> "serious" companies eventually graduate to running their own models. Plenty of large,
> sophisticated companies use frontier models via API forever, because managing a model
> in-house isn't their business. The right call depends on your volume, your data
> sensitivity, and whether you have anyone whose job it would be to run it.

## One honest note on model size

As Day 1 covered, a bigger parameter count doesn't mean a better model, and the count itself
is often not even public. Open-weight labs typically publish the real parameter count for
each model, because part of the point of open-weighting a model is letting people inspect
and run it themselves. Frontier labs like Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google generally don't
publish parameter counts for their latest models at all — it's treated as a competitive
detail, not a marketing number. Don't expect a specific figure from a frontier vendor, and
don't take a competitor's guess as fact either.

## How to actually compare models

Once you get past parameter count, four things actually tell you something.

**Benchmarks and evals.** Researchers test models against standardised sets of questions and
tasks — called **[evals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_model_benchmark)** — and
publish the scores. These are a useful first filter, but they measure general skill on
generic tasks, not your task. Day 15 covers how to read and build evals properly; for now,
just know a benchmark score is a starting point, not a verdict.

**Price per token.** As Day 1 covered, everything you send and receive is billed in tokens.
Vendors publish a price per million tokens, split between what you send in (input) and what
the model sends back (output) — and output is usually priced higher, because it costs more
to generate. Always compare on this basis, not on a vague "per message" or "per seat" claim.

**Context window size.** Also from Day 1 — how much text a model can hold in one
conversation. If your task involves feeding in long documents, contracts, or codebases, this
number can matter more than raw capability.

**Testing it on your own task.** This is the one that actually settles the question. A
headline benchmark score tells you how a model does on someone else's test. Running your own
real example — your actual support ticket, your actual contract clause, your actual
spreadsheet — tells you how it does on yours. No comparison table replaces this step.

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. A vendor's slide claims their model "outperforms the competition," with a single
   benchmark chart as proof. Ask: *"Which benchmark is that, and does it reflect the kind of
   task we'd actually be using this for?"* — separates a genuine capability claim from a
   cherry-picked chart.
2. In a budget meeting, someone proposes running an open-weight model in-house to "cut AI
   costs." Ask: *"Once we add the infrastructure and the team to run it, is that actually
   cheaper than what we're paying per token today?"* — makes sure the comparison includes
   the real cost of self-hosting, not just the licence being free.
3. During a vendor pitch, someone asks how big the model is, and the vendor gives a vague or
   evasive answer. Ask: *"Is parameter count something you publish, or is that not something
   frontier labs disclose?"* — shows you already know closed-weight labs typically don't
   publish this, so a dodge doesn't read as a red flag by itself.
4. In a rollout planning meeting, the team is choosing between two shortlisted models based
   only on their spec sheets. Ask: *"Before we decide, can we run both models on three or
   four real examples from our own work and compare the actual output?"* — replaces a
   paper comparison with the one test that actually predicts how the model will perform for
   you.

You don't need to become an engineer today. You just need enough to follow a sentence like
"we fine-tuned a 70-billion-parameter model with a 200K context window" — and know which
part of that actually matters.

## The one idea that explains everything

An AI chatbot like ChatGPT or Claude is, underneath, a very good guessing machine. Give it
some text, and it guesses the single most likely next word. Then it adds that word to the
text and guesses again. And again. That's it — that's the whole trick, repeated thousands
of times per answer.

Everything the model seems to "know how to do" — writing an email, explaining a spreadsheet
formula, holding a conversation — comes from doing that one guessing trick extremely well,
at a huge scale. Keep this in mind, because most confusing AI claims fall apart once you
remember this is all it's doing.

## Token: the model's unit of text

A model doesn't read words the way you do. It breaks text into small pieces called
**[tokens](https://platform.openai.com/tokenizer)** — roughly a word, sometimes a bit less.

Think of it like a vending machine that only accepts small coins, not notes. If you hand it
a big idea (a sentence), it breaks it down into small coins (tokens) it knows how to
process one at a time.

- "The server is overheating" is about 5 tokens.
- An unusual word like "Omnivise" gets split into two or three odd fragments, because the
  model hasn't seen that exact word often.
- Text in Hindi, Tamil, or German typically breaks into 2–3 times more tokens than the same
  sentence in English.

> **Why this matters to you:** tokens are what you pay for. Every AI bill, every "message
> limit," every "document too long" error is really a token limit in disguise. If your
> customers write in a language other than English, your AI costs are quietly higher than
> a competitor serving only English speakers — and no sales pitch will mention that.

## Parameters: the model's learned settings

**[Parameters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_network_(machine_learning)#Parameters)**
(also called "weights") are the internal settings a model tunes while it's learning. A model
described as having "70 billion parameters" has 70 billion of these internal dials, each
adjusted automatically during training to make its guesses better.

A simple comparison: think of a giant mixing desk with billions of tiny knobs, each nudged
slightly by trial and error until the output sounds right. Nobody sat down and set each knob
by hand, and nobody can point at one knob and say exactly what it does. That's very
different from a simple setting you'd adjust yourself, like a thermostat.

**The trap to avoid:** a bigger parameter count doesn't automatically mean a better model. A
smaller model trained well often beats a bigger model trained poorly. Many modern models
also only use a portion of their total parameters for any single answer (a design called
**[Mixture-of-Experts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixture_of_experts)**) — so "600 billion
parameters" and "600 billion parameters working on your question right now" can be very
different claims. When a vendor leads with a big parameter number instead of results on your
task, that's usually a sign they'd rather not talk about performance.

## Training vs. using the model — two very different costs

This is the single most useful thing to understand as a leader, because training and using
an AI model cost money in completely different ways — and mixing them up is how AI budgets
go wrong.

<TrainingInferenceDiagram />

**Training happens once, like building a factory.** A company gathers a huge amount of text,
then runs an enormous, expensive computing job — often costing tens to hundreds of millions
of dollars — to produce the finished model. Only a handful of companies in the world do
this. Afterwards, the model doesn't change. It's frozen, like a book that's already been
printed.

**Using the model happens every single time someone sends it a message**, the way running a
factory costs money every day it's open, separately from the cost of building it. And here's
the part that explains a lot of what you see on screen: the model writes its answer one
token at a time, feeding each word back in to help guess the next one — that's why answers
appear gradually, word by word, instead of all at once. It also explains why replies cost
more than questions: your question is processed in one go, but the model's reply has to be
built one small step at a time.

The detail most leaders miss: **the model doesn't learn from talking to you.** It doesn't
remember your last conversation, and it won't recognise you tomorrow. If a chatbot seems to
"remember" a customer's order or your company's policies, that's not the model learning —
that's separate software feeding it reminders each time (more on this on Day 9). Any vendor
who implies the model itself is quietly learning about your business is either confused
about their own product, or hoping you won't ask.

## Two more terms worth knowing

**Knowledge cutoff** — the date the model's training data stops. Its built-in knowledge is
like an encyclopedia printed on a specific date: ask about something that happened after
that date, and it either says it doesn't know, or — more riskily — makes up a plausible-
sounding but wrong answer.

**[Context window](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_language_model#Context_window)** —
how much text the model can "hold in mind" during one conversation, usually enough for a few
hundred pages. This is the fix for the knowledge-cutoff problem: instead of relying on what
the model already knows, you paste in the current document, and it reads and reasons over
that instead.

A simple way to remember the difference: training data is like long-term memory — fixed, and
not always reliable on specifics. The context window is like a notepad you hand the model
right before asking your question — fresh, accurate, and something you control. We'll build
on this notepad idea when we cover a technique called RAG on Day 9.

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. A vendor's demo just answered a detailed question about your own company's pricing
   sheet, and everyone in the room looks impressed. Ask: *"Did we paste that pricing sheet
   in during this demo, or does the model somehow already know it?"* — tells you whether
   you're looking at real capability or a rehearsed trick, before you sign anything.
2. Someone on your team says a pilot "only cost a few dollars to test." Ask: *"What would
   that cost per thousand customer messages, at the volume we'd actually run in
   production?"* — moves the conversation from a cheap demo to the real monthly bill.
3. A vendor claims their assistant "knows our industry inside and out." Ask: *"Since the
   model itself doesn't update after training, where is that industry knowledge actually
   coming from — and how current is it?"* — quickly exposes vague or overstated claims
   about what the AI genuinely knows versus what's being fed to it.
4. In a planning meeting, someone pushes for "the biggest model available" as the safe
   default. Ask: *"Instead of picking by size, can we see how each option actually scores
   on a task like ours?"* — redirects the decision from parameter count to real
   performance, and sets up Day 15, where we cover how to measure AI quality properly.

You already know the basics of AI pricing: tokens are the billing unit (Day 1), and vendors
charge a rate per million tokens, split between input and output, with output costing more
because it's generated one step at a time (Day 1, Day 4). Today builds on that. It's about
what actually makes an AI bill bigger or smaller in practice — and the two biggest levers a
team controls: caching and picking the right-sized model for each task.

## An AI bill isn't a fixed cost — it's a series of choices

It's easy to treat an AI bill the way you'd treat an electricity bill: a number that arrives
each month, driven mostly by how much you used the thing. AI costs don't work quite like
that. The same workload, built two different ways, can cost very different amounts — because
the bill is shaped by engineering decisions, not just usage volume.

Three of those decisions matter most: which model handles which task, whether repeated
context is cached instead of reprocessed every time, and how much "thinking time" a model is
allowed to spend per question (Day 6). A team that hasn't looked at any of these is very
likely paying more than it needs to — not because AI is inherently expensive, but because the
default settings are rarely the cheapest ones.

## Caching: don't pay to re-read the same thing twice

Many AI tools send the same large chunk of unchanging context with every single request — a
long system prompt describing how the assistant should behave, a company handbook, a lengthy
document the user is asking several questions about. Without caching, the model reprocesses
that entire chunk from scratch on every single call, and you pay full price for it every
time, even though nothing in it changed.

**[Caching](https://docs.anthropic.com/en/docs/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)** lets a
provider reuse the processing work already done on a piece of context that hasn't changed,
instead of redoing it from zero. The part of the request that repeats gets billed at a much
lower rate on the calls after the first one.

A simple comparison: a restaurant kitchen making a big batch of sauce once in the morning,
rather than cooking a fresh batch from raw ingredients for every single order. The first
batch takes real time and effort. Every order after that just draws from what's already
made — faster, and far cheaper per order.

> **Why this matters to you:** if your AI tool sends the same large context — a handbook, a
> style guide, a long system prompt — with every request, caching can meaningfully cut what
> you're paying, with no change to what the assistant actually does. This is a concrete,
> answerable question for an engineering team: "are we using caching for the context we
> repeat on every call?" If the answer is no, that's often the single easiest cost fix
> available, before touching anything else.

## Model right-sizing: stop using a sledgehammer on everything

Day 1 already made the point that a bigger model isn't automatically a better one. Day 6
showed that "thinking time" is a separate, controllable cost dial, not a fixed property of a
model. Day 11 taught matching the technique — prompting, [RAG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrieval-augmented_generation),
or fine-tuning — to the actual need instead of reaching for the fanciest option by default.
**Model right-sizing** is the same discipline, applied specifically to cost: use the
cheapest, smallest model that's actually good enough for a given task, rather than routing
every task through your most powerful, most expensive model by default.

A simple task — classifying an incoming email, pulling a date out of a form, tagging a
support ticket by topic — often doesn't need your most capable model at all. A smaller,
cheaper model frequently handles it just as well, with no noticeable difference in quality
for that specific job. Save the most expensive model for the tasks that genuinely need its
extra capability — a nuanced piece of writing, a complex analysis, a judgment call with real
stakes.

> **Why this matters to you:** a business running one expensive frontier model for
> absolutely everything — simple lookups and hard judgment calls alike — is very likely
> overpaying, in the same way it would be wasteful to hire your most senior, highest-paid
> specialist to do every routine task in the building. Right-sizing isn't about using worse
> AI. It's about not paying frontier prices for work that doesn't require frontier
> capability.

## Putting the levers together

None of this requires becoming an engineer. It requires knowing that "the AI bill" is not one
fixed number handed down from a vendor — it's the sum of choices a team makes: how much
context gets sent on every call (Day 3), whether that repeated context is cached, how much
thinking time is spent per question (Day 6), and which model is doing the work. Change any of
those, and the bill changes, often without changing what the product actually does for the
user.

That means a leader who understands tokenomics can ask a sharper question in a budget review
than "how much does the AI bill cost." The sharper version is: "is our AI bill actually
optimized, or are we just paying the default rate for everything?" Before accepting a large
or growing AI bill as simply the cost of doing business, it's worth asking whether the team
has actually looked at right-sizing, caching, and unused thinking time — the concrete levers
covered today, not vague cost-cutting ideas.

**Where this heads at a larger scale: FinOps for AI.** Everything above is about levers one
engineering team can pull on one project. Once a company has AI running across several teams
and products, a related question shows up: who's actually watching total AI spend across the
whole business, forecasting it, and deciding how it's allocated between teams — the same
organizational discipline that cloud computing costs eventually grew into its own function
for, sometimes called FinOps. Most companies aren't there yet, and don't need to be for a
first AI project. But if your AI spend is starting to show up as a real line item across
multiple teams, treating it with the same ongoing budgeting discipline as any other shared
infrastructure cost — not just optimizing one project's bill in isolation — is worth planning
for before it becomes a surprise.

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. Finance flags that the AI bill has grown every month this quarter, and the team's answer
   is "we're just using it more." Ask: *"Beyond usage growing, have we looked at whether
   we're using the right-sized model for each task, or defaulting to the most expensive one
   everywhere?"* — separates genuine growth from an unoptimized setup that's quietly costing
   more than it needs to.
2. An engineer mentions the product sends a long system prompt or reference document with
   every single request. Ask: *"Is that repeated content being cached, or are we
   reprocessing it from scratch on every call?"* — surfaces one of the simplest, highest-
   leverage cost fixes available, without touching the product experience.
3. In a planning meeting, someone proposes routing every feature — including simple ones
   like tagging or classification — through the same top-tier model "for consistency." Ask:
   *"Which of these tasks are simple enough that a smaller, cheaper model would do just as
   well?"* — turns a blanket, expensive default into a deliberate, task-by-task decision.
4. A vendor pitching an AI platform quotes one flat price per query, regardless of task
   complexity. Ask: *"Does this route simple requests to a cheaper model automatically, or
   are we paying the same rate whether the task is trivial or complex?"* — reveals whether
   the vendor has already built in right-sizing, or whether that cost discipline is left
   entirely to you.

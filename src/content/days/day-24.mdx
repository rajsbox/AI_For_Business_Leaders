Back on Day 9, we ended with an honest caveat: RAG is only as good as what it retrieves. Today
takes that one step further, because it's not really about the retrieval mechanism at all —
it's about what's sitting on the other end of it. The best model in the world, wired up
perfectly, still can't answer well from documents that are missing, outdated, contradictory,
or unreadable. Today is about that ceiling, and how to actually check for it.

## The idea in one sentence

Your company's data — its quality, organization, and accessibility — sets a hard ceiling on
how good any AI system built on top of it can be. No amount of model quality or clever
prompting fixes that. A brilliant model handed messy data still produces messy answers; it
will just say them more fluently and more confidently than a worse model would.

This is the direct, practical follow-through on Day 9's caveat and Day 10's "search by
meaning": a RAG system can only find what's actually there to be found, described clearly
enough to be found, and correct once it's found. Everything below is about what "actually
there, findable, and correct" really requires.

## Four questions that decide whether your data is AI-ready

These aren't abstract IT concerns — they're things a leader can genuinely check by asking
around, before approving a project.

**Is it findable?** Relevant information that's scattered across someone's inbox, a personal
laptop, sticky notes, or an old unindexed folder isn't searchable by a retrieval system, no
matter how good that system is. As Day 10 covered, retrieval works by comparing meaning across
a defined set of documents — but it can only search what's actually been put somewhere it can
reach. Information that exists but can't be reached might as well not exist at all, for AI
purposes.

**Is it current?** A stale document — an old policy that was replaced six months ago but never
removed from the shared drive — gets retrieved and used just as confidently as the correct,
current version. Day 16 covered grounding: basing an answer on retrieved source material
rather than the model's own guesswork. Grounding in the *wrong* document is arguably worse
than not grounding at all, because the answer looks just as authoritative either way. A reader
has no way to tell, from the answer alone, whether it came from this year's policy or last
year's.

**Is it consistent?** Do different documents contradict each other — two versions of the same
policy still both in circulation, or two reports with different numbers for the same thing?
A retrieval system has no way to know which one is "right." It just finds something relevant
and hands it over. If your own documents disagree with each other, the AI will happily inherit
that disagreement and state one side of it with total confidence.

**Is it accessible in the right way?** Some genuinely important information is locked in
formats that are hard to work with — a scanned image of a document with no searchable text
behind it, or an old system nobody can cleanly export data from. As Day 5 covered,
vision-style tools can read scanned documents and images, but that's an extra processing step
that has to be deliberately built in — it doesn't happen automatically just because the
document technically exists somewhere.

> **Why this matters to you:** these four checks are the practical version of "is our data
> ready for AI?" You don't need to inspect a single document yourself to ask about all four —
> you just need to know these are the right four things to ask about.

## The reframe: "data cleanup" is an AI investment, not IT housekeeping

"Let's get our documents organized" sounds like unglamorous back-office work, easy to
deprioritize behind a flashier AI feature. In practice, it's often one of the highest-leverage
AI investments a company can make — frequently more impactful than switching to a fancier
model, because it directly raises the ceiling described above. A mediocre model on excellent,
current, consistent data will usually outperform an excellent model on messy data.

This also connects directly to Day 15's lesson on evals. A team can build a genuinely
rigorous, systematic evaluation process and still get mediocre scores — correctly — because
the documents being retrieved from are bad. The eval isn't wrong in that case; it's doing its
job, catching the problem. But the fix isn't a better model or a fancier prompt. It's fixing
the data the system is actually pulling from.

**The trap to avoid:** treating a low eval score as proof the model needs replacing. Before
reaching for a bigger or newer model, check whether the retrieved source material was even
correct and current to begin with. Swapping models won't fix a filing cabinet full of
outdated pages.

## What to ask before approving a data-heavy AI project

Before signing off on a RAG-based or knowledge-heavy AI project, it's fair — and useful — to
ask about the state of the underlying data, the same way you'd ask about budget or timeline:

- Is the source material centralized, or scattered across many places?
- How current is it, and when was it last reviewed?
- Has anyone actually checked it for contradictions between documents?
- Is there a named owner responsible for keeping it accurate *after* launch, not just at
  launch?

That last one matters most, and gets skipped most often. A data set can be perfectly clean on
launch day and quietly stale within a few months if nobody owns keeping it that way — which
brings the ceiling right back down, no matter how well the project started.

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. A team is proposing a RAG-based assistant over your company's policy documents, and the
   conversation is all about which model to use. Ask: *"Before we pick a model, how confident
   are we in the documents it'll be pulling from — are they centralized, current, and free of
   contradictions?"* — redirects the conversation to the actual ceiling on quality, before
   money gets spent on the wrong lever.
2. An eval report comes back showing a new AI tool scoring poorly on accuracy. Ask: *"Is this
   the model reasoning badly, or is it working from outdated or contradictory source
   documents?"* — separates a model problem from a data problem, which are fixed in completely
   different ways.
3. In a budget conversation, "document cleanup" is proposed as a separate, lower-priority line
   item from the AI project itself. Ask: *"Since the AI can't be better than the data it reads,
   should this actually be treated as part of the AI budget, not a nice-to-have alongside it?"*
   — makes the connection between data quality and AI quality explicit before priorities get
   set.
4. A rollout is being planned for an AI tool built on a shared drive full of older documents.
   Ask: *"Who owns keeping this data current after launch, so we're not back here in six
   months with the same stale-answer problem?"* — surfaces whether there's a real long-term
   owner, or whether the project assumes the data will just stay accurate on its own.

Back on Day 3, we compared the context window to a notepad you hand the model right before
asking your question — fresh and accurate, unlike its fixed training data. Today's technique
is how that notepad actually gets filled in most real business tools, automatically, with
the right pages. It's called RAG, and it's the pattern behind almost every serious AI product
built for a company's own data.

## What RAG actually is

**[Retrieval-Augmented Generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrieval-augmented_generation)**,
or RAG, means the model doesn't just answer from what it learned during training. Instead,
before it answers, the system first *retrieves* relevant material from your own documents or
database, and adds that material to the context window alongside your question. The model
then answers using what was just retrieved — your current, real information — not only its
trained-in memory.

That's the whole idea. Two things happen instead of one: a search step, then an answer step.

## The filing cabinet analogy

Picture a smart assistant who, before answering your question, walks over to your company's
filing cabinet, pulls out the few most relevant pages, reads just those, and answers based on
what's actually written there. That's very different from an assistant answering purely from
memory of things they skimmed once, a long time ago, and may have half-forgotten or gotten
slightly wrong since.

RAG is that first assistant. The "memory" is the model's training. The "walk to the filing
cabinet" is the retrieval step. The model still writes the answer — but it's now writing from
notes it just pulled, not from recollection alone.

## The basic flow

<RagFlowDiagram />

1. **Your question comes in** — the same as any normal AI interaction.
2. **The system searches your own documents or database** for the most relevant pieces. We'll
   cover exactly how that search works — a technique called embeddings and vector
   search — on Day 10. For today, it's enough to know it's a search step, not a guess.
3. **Those relevant pieces get added into the context window**, sitting right alongside your
   question — the notepad from Day 3, now filled in automatically by the search step instead
   of by a person copy-pasting.
4. **The model answers using that retrieved material**, the same way it would answer about
   any document you'd pasted in yourself.

## Why it's called "the #1 enterprise pattern"

Most real business AI tools work this way: a company chatbot that knows your HR policies, a
support bot that knows your product documentation, an internal assistant that can answer
questions about your own contracts or reports. All of these are RAG systems under the hood.

The reason is straightforward. As you saw on Day 1, a model's training has a **knowledge
cutoff** — a fixed date after which it knows nothing — and it was never trained on your
company's internal documents in the first place, no matter how recent its cutoff is. A
business needs answers grounded in *its own* current, specific information, not the model's
generic trained knowledge. RAG is the standard way to bridge that gap without touching the
model itself.

> **Why this matters to you:** when a vendor demo answers a detailed question about your
> company correctly, that's almost never the model "knowing" your business. It's RAG — the
> demo retrieved a document behind the scenes and fed it in. Worth remembering the next time
> a demo looks like magic.

## Why this beats retraining the model

There's another way to give a model your company's knowledge: retrain it on your own data, a
process called **fine-tuning**. We'll compare the two properly on Day 11, but the short
version is that RAG is usually far cheaper and faster to set up. Updating a RAG system means
updating your documents — adding a new policy PDF, editing a page — not retraining a model.
That also means your AI tool's knowledge can be kept current on an ordinary schedule, the same
way you'd update a shared drive, rather than requiring a specialised retraining job every time
something changes.

## The honest caveat: retrieval is the bottleneck

RAG is only as good as what it retrieves. If the search step in step 2 pulls the wrong or
irrelevant pages — an outdated policy, a document about the wrong product, a page that's
merely similar-sounding rather than actually relevant — the model will still write a
confident, fluent answer. It just won't be a *correct* one, because it's confidently
summarising the wrong material.

**The trap to avoid:** assuming that if a RAG-based tool gives a wrong answer, the fix is
"use a smarter model." Often the model behaved perfectly given what it was handed — the real
problem is upstream, in what got retrieved. Retrieval quality is the actual bottleneck in most
RAG systems, not the model's intelligence, which is exactly why Day 10's topic — how that
search step really works — matters for anyone evaluating one of these tools.

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. A vendor's demo just answered a detailed question about your company's own policy
   document, and the room is impressed. Ask: *"Is that RAG pulling from a document we gave
   you, or is the model somehow supposed to already know that?"* — separates a real,
   repeatable capability from a rehearsed one-off demo.
2. In a project review, an engineer proposes fine-tuning a model on your company's data
   instead of building retrieval. Ask: *"Why retrain the model instead of just letting it
   retrieve from our documents — what does fine-tuning get us that RAG doesn't?"* — makes
   sure the harder, more expensive path is a deliberate choice, not a default.
3. A support bot built on your product documentation gives a confidently wrong answer. Ask:
   *"Was that the model reasoning badly, or did it retrieve the wrong document to begin
   with?"* — points the debugging effort at the actual likely cause, retrieval, not the model.
4. During a rollout planning meeting, someone asks how the AI tool's knowledge will stay
   current after launch. Ask: *"When our policies change next quarter, do we retrain
   anything, or just update the source documents?"* — surfaces whether the team has actually
   built this as a RAG system with a simple update path, before you commit to it.

You've probably noticed some AI tools now show a "thinking" step before they answer, and
that a few even let you pick "fast" versus "extended thinking." Today explains what's
actually happening there, and why it's not just a loading animation — it's a real business
decision with a real cost attached.

## Blurting out an answer vs. working it out

As Day 1 covered, a model builds its answer one word at a time, guessing the next word
based on everything before it. A standard model does this immediately — it starts guessing
the moment it sees your question, the same way someone might blurt out the first answer
that comes to mind.

A **reasoning model** (also called a "thinking" model) does something different. Before it
gives you a final answer, it first generates a chunk of intermediate reasoning — working
through the problem step by step, checking itself, trying an approach and backing out of it
if it doesn't lead anywhere. Only after that does it write the answer it actually shows you.

A simple way to picture the difference: it's the difference between someone who calls out
the first move that looks good in a chess game, and someone who sits quietly, plays out a
few moves in their head, notices one leads to a trap, and only then makes their move. Both
players eventually move a piece. One of them thought it through first.

## Test-time compute: a third dial, not just a bigger model

Day 1 drew a hard line between two costs: training a model once (expensive, happens up
front) and running it every time someone asks it something (inference). For a long time,
those were basically the only two levers a business had — how big the model is, and how it
was trained.

**Test-time compute** is a third lever, and it's a genuinely new one: instead of only asking
"how big is the model" or "how was it trained," you can now also ask "how much thinking
time do we give it on this specific question, right now, at the moment we ask it." The model
itself doesn't get bigger or retrained — it's the same model. What changes is how much
computation it's allowed to spend working through your particular question before it
answers.

This matters because it decouples model quality from model size for the first time. Instead
of the only way to get a better answer being "buy access to a bigger, more expensive model,"
you can sometimes get a better answer from the *same* model, on the *same* question, simply
by letting it think longer.

## Why this changed the economics

Before reasoning models, the cost of asking a model a question was fairly predictable — it
scaled roughly with how long the answer was. Now, cost also scales with how much *thinking*
happened before the answer, which the model itself decides based on the difficulty of the
problem, or which you can sometimes explicitly control.

That creates a real tradeoff that didn't clearly exist before: more thinking time tends to
produce a better, more reliable answer on hard problems — but it also costs more and takes
longer to get that answer back. This isn't a fixed cost per model anymore. It's a dial you
can turn up or down, per question, depending on how much the answer matters.

> **Why this matters to you:** a business can now deliberately spend more "thinking" on a
> hard, high-stakes question — a contract clause, a tricky piece of code, a multi-step
> financial analysis — and spend less on a simple one, like drafting a routine email. That's
> a genuinely new kind of decision. Before, you mostly just picked a model and lived with its
> fixed cost and speed. Now you're also choosing how hard you want it to work on this
> particular question.

## Matching the tool to the task

The temptation is to assume "thinking" is always better, and switch everything over to a
reasoning model by default. It usually isn't worth it. Extra thinking time helps most on
problems that genuinely have multiple steps or require weighing tradeoffs — planning a
multi-part project, debugging a subtle problem, working through a dense legal or financial
document. On those, the extra time is usually worth the extra cost.

On simple, well-defined tasks — drafting a routine email, looking up a fact, reformatting a
document — extra thinking time buys you very little. The model would likely land on the same
answer either way, just slower and for more money. Using a reasoning model here is like
asking someone to play out ten chess moves in their head before deciding what to have for
lunch: the deliberation doesn't make the decision any better, it just makes it slower.

This is the same lesson from earlier in the course — match the tool to the task — applied to
a new lever. The skill isn't "always use the thinking model" or "never use it." It's knowing
which of your questions are actually hard enough to deserve the extra time, and which ones
aren't.

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. A vendor demos their new "reasoning" model and it visibly pauses before answering, and
   the room seems impressed by the pause itself. Ask: *"Is this task actually hard enough to
   need that extra thinking time, or would a faster model give us the same answer for less?"*
   — stops "it looks thoughtful" from being mistaken for "it's necessary."
2. Your team is comparing two AI tools and one is noticeably slower and pricier per
   response. Ask: *"Is that because it's a bigger model, or because it's spending more time
   thinking per question — and can we control how much thinking it does?"* — separates a
   model-size cost from a per-question thinking-time cost, which are different problems with
   different fixes.
3. In a budget conversation, someone proposes switching every AI-powered feature in the
   product to a reasoning model "to improve quality across the board." Ask: *"Which of these
   features are actually solving multi-step problems, and which are simple lookups that
   won't benefit from the extra thinking time?"* — turns a blanket upgrade into a targeted
   one, and avoids paying reasoning-model prices for tasks that don't need it.
4. During a rollout planning meeting, someone asks why the AI assistant feels slower than
   the demo they saw last month. Ask: *"Did we turn on extended thinking for this, and if
   so, is it actually being used on the hard questions, or on everything by default?"* —
   catches a common misconfiguration where thinking time is switched on globally instead of
   only where it earns its cost.

Most people's first experience of AI is a text chat box — you type, it types back. But the
same kind of model can now also look at a photo, listen to audio, or generate a picture or a
video clip. Today is about knowing which of those you can actually rely on, and which ones
are still mostly a good show.

## What "multimodal" actually means

A "mode" here just means a type of input or output: text, images, audio, or video. A
**[multimodal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multimodal_learning)** AI system is one that can
work with more than one of these, instead of only text.

Think of it like a colleague who used to only reply to emails, and can now also glance at a
photo you send them, listen to a voice note, and describe what they see or hear back to you
in writing. Same colleague, same underlying judgment — just more ways to send information in
and get it out.

This matters for a simple reason: every modality is not equally mature. A model can be
excellent at reading text and still be shaky at reliably understanding a blurry photo, or
generating a five-second video clip that holds together. Treating "multimodal" as one single
capability, all equally trustworthy, is where a lot of AI projects quietly go wrong.

## The four modalities, honestly ranked by maturity

<ModalityMaturityDiagram />

**Text** is the most mature by a wide margin. Reading and writing text is what these models
were built to do first, and it's been reliable enough for real business use for a while now —
drafting, summarising, answering questions from documents.

**Vision** — a model looking at an image and describing or reasoning about what's in it — is
quite mature and genuinely useful today. Reading a scanned invoice, pulling numbers off a
photographed whiteboard, or explaining what a chart in a slide is showing are all things
current models do well and consistently enough to build a real process around.

**Voice** is a mixed picture. Turning speech into text, and turning text into spoken audio,
are both mature and dependable — this is the same underlying technology behind
[voice assistants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_recognition) that have existed for
years. But a model holding a natural, real-time spoken *conversation* — listening,
understanding, and responding out loud with no awkward pauses or misunderstandings — is much
newer, and noticeably less consistent than a straightforward transcription task.

**Video** is the least mature of the four. Generating a video clip, or having a model deeply
understand a long video the way it can a document, produces some genuinely impressive demos —
but reliability, cost, and consistency all lag well behind text and vision. A demo video that
looks flawless once is not the same as that same task working reliably, at your volume, every
single time.

> **Why this matters to you:** the more visually impressive a modality is, the more likely a
> vendor is to lead with a heavily rehearsed, cherry-picked example of it. Flashy output is
> not evidence of reliability — it's evidence that, at least once, under ideal conditions, it
> worked.

**A related question worth asking, especially as image and video generation improve: how do
you tell AI-generated content apart from real content?** The honest answer today is
"imperfectly." Some tools embed a **watermark** — a signal inside the file marking it as
AI-generated — and some platforms are starting to label AI content automatically, but there's
no universal, reliable standard yet, and a determined person can often strip these signals
out. If your business publishes anything generated with these tools, or needs to verify that
something coming in from outside wasn't AI-generated (a deepfake used in a scam attempt, for
instance), treat that as an open, evolving problem — not a solved one you can assume a vendor
has already handled for you.

## The core lesson: the flashiest demo is the least representative one

Here's the pattern worth remembering: a vendor's *most impressive* demo is usually built
around the *least mature* modality, because that's where the "wow" factor lives. A text
chatbot answering questions well doesn't turn heads anymore. A generated video does. That's
exactly backwards from how much you should trust it.

Think of it like a restaurant showing you photos of their food on the menu. The dish that
looks most spectacular in the photo is often the one most different from what actually
arrives at your table. The plain, ordinary-looking dish is usually closer to what you'll
really get.

**The trap to avoid:** watching one striking video-generation or live-voice demo and assuming
that quality is what you'll get every time, on your own material, at your own volume. Ask to
see it run on *your* data, live and unscripted, not a rehearsed example the vendor has run
many times to get right. This single question matters more here than almost anywhere else in
this course, precisely because video and voice are the easiest modalities to make look
flawless once and hardest to make reliable every time.

## Matching modality maturity to business risk

Not every task needs the most mature modality — but the riskier the task, the less room you
have to experiment with an immature one.

A low-stakes internal tool — say, a draft generator only your own team sees before editing —
can tolerate a less mature modality. If a voice-to-text draft note comes out slightly wrong
occasionally, someone catches it before it goes anywhere.

A customer-facing or compliance-relevant task is a different story. If a customer is relying
on a live voice assistant to get their account details right, or a generated video is
representing your brand publicly, you're now exposed to whatever that modality's current
weak points are. Default to the most mature option for the job — usually text, or vision for
document-based tasks — unless you've specifically tested the newer modality on your own real
cases, at your own scale, and it held up.

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. A vendor plays a slick generated video during their pitch, and the room goes quiet with
   admiration. Ask: *"Can we see this run live, right now, on something from our own
   business — not a clip you've already produced?"* — separates a rehearsed showcase from a
   capability you can actually depend on.
2. Someone proposes a customer-facing voice assistant to replace part of your call centre.
   Ask: *"Is this reliable, natural conversation, or is it closer to scripted
   question-and-answer with a voice layered on top?"* — surfaces whether you're buying mature
   transcription technology or the far less proven real-time conversation capability.
3. In a budget meeting, a team wants funding for a tool that reads scanned paperwork using AI
   vision. Ask: *"Have we tested this on our actual documents — the messy, real ones — not
   the clean sample set in the vendor's demo?"* — checks that a genuinely mature modality is
   being applied to your real conditions, not just praised in the abstract.
4. During rollout planning, someone suggests using AI-generated video for external marketing
   on a tight deadline. Ask: *"Given video is the least mature of these modalities right now,
   what's our fallback if it doesn't hold up at the volume and consistency we need?"* — makes
   sure a demo-stage capability isn't quietly load-bearing for a customer-facing deadline.

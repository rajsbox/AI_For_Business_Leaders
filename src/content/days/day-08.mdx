Week 1 was about understanding AI as a reader or user of it. Starting today, Week 2 shifts
altitude: we're looking at how AI actually gets *built into* a product — the pieces an
engineer assembles when your company builds, or buys, an "AI-powered" feature. You don't
need to write any of this yourself. You need to recognise the three pieces well enough to
ask about them.

## What an API actually is

When you type into ChatGPT's website, you're a human clicking through a chat box. But when a
company builds "AI-powered" into their own product — a support widget, a report generator, a
search bar — there's usually no chat box at all. Their software sends a request directly to
the AI company's servers, gets an answer back, and displays it inside their own product. You
never see any of that happen.

That direct, software-to-software connection is called an
**[API](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/API)** — short for application programming interface.
Think of it like ordering through a waiter at a restaurant. You never walk into the kitchen;
you tell the waiter what you want, the waiter relays it to the kitchen, and brings back the
finished dish. The API is the waiter: it carries a request in, and carries a result back out,
without you or the end customer ever seeing what happened in between.

This matters because it's the difference between "we use ChatGPT sometimes" and "our product
is built on top of an AI model." The first is a person using a website. The second is a
piece of software your company built, quietly calling someone else's model every time a
customer uses a feature — and every one of those calls is billed, logged, and shaped by
choices your team made.

## The system prompt: the product's hidden personality

Every time that software sends a request to the model, it doesn't just send the customer's
message. It first attaches its own hidden instructions, called a
**[system prompt](https://www.promptingguide.ai/introduction/settings)** — written once by
whoever built the product, sent silently ahead of every single message, and never shown to
the end user.

A system prompt might read something like: *"You are a customer support assistant for Acme
Corp. Only answer questions about our products. Be concise. Never discuss competitors."* The
customer just sees a helpful, on-brand assistant that stays on topic — they have no idea
those rules exist, or that a different company using the exact same underlying model could
have written completely different instructions and gotten a completely different-feeling
product.

This is the biggest thing to internalise from today: **two products can use the identical AI
model and behave nothing alike**, purely because of what's written in their system prompt.
When a chatbot on one company's website is chatty and casual, and another is terse and
formal, that's usually not a different model — it's a different set of hidden instructions
sitting in front of the same one.

## Temperature: the creativity dial

The third piece is a setting called **[temperature](https://platform.openai.com/docs/api-reference/chat/create#chat-create-temperature)**,
which controls how predictable or how varied the model's answers are.

Picture asking the same knowledgeable colleague the same question many times. At low
temperature, they give you their single most confident, safest answer, worded almost the
same way every time. At high temperature, they're more willing to take a chance — to phrase
things unusually, offer a surprising angle, or suggest something less obvious. Sometimes
that's a genuinely better answer. Sometimes it's a worse one. High temperature trades
consistency for variety, in both directions.

That trade-off decides where each setting belongs. A low temperature suits places where
customers expect the same correct answer every time — looking up a return policy, answering
a factual question, generating a report from data. A high temperature suits places where
variety is the actual point — brainstorming marketing taglines, drafting several different
opening lines for an email, exploring options rather than settling on one. There's no single
"correct" temperature; it's a deliberate choice for the job the feature is doing, made by
whoever built it.

## Putting the three together

An API, a system prompt, and a temperature setting are the three levers that were actually
set by someone, every time a business builds or buys an AI-powered feature. None of them are
visible to the end user. All of them were decided by a person on the building side — and all
three can be changed without changing the underlying model at all.

This reframes how you should hear the phrase "it uses AI." That sentence tells you almost
nothing on its own. The real product is the specific combination: which model is being
called through the API, what hidden instructions are steering it, and how much freedom it's
been given to vary its answers. A leader who can ask about all three is evaluating the actual
product in front of them, not just trusting a label.

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. A vendor demos a support chatbot that stays perfectly on-brand and never wanders off
   topic. Ask: *"What's in the system prompt that's keeping it this disciplined — and can we
   see it, or at least edit it?"* — surfaces whether the vendor actually controls the
   product's behaviour, or is just hoping the model behaves.
2. Two teams built similar-sounding AI features, but one feels much more consistent than the
   other. Ask: *"What temperature setting is each one running at?"* — turns a vague "this
   one feels better" into a concrete, adjustable setting worth comparing.
3. In a budget conversation, someone says "we're just using the AI API, it's simple." Ask:
   *"Every one of our customers' messages is going out over that API and coming back billed
   — do we know our real request volume?"* — reframes an API call from something invisible
   into a metered cost that scales with usage.
4. During a rollout planning meeting, a team proposes reusing one AI assistant for both
   factual customer lookups and creative marketing copy. Ask: *"Doesn't that need two
   different temperature settings — and maybe two different system prompts — rather than one
   assistant trying to do both?"* — catches a design that quietly forces one setting to serve
   two conflicting jobs.

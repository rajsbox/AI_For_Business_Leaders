## The whole course in two paragraphs

Week 1 was about understanding what this technology actually is, underneath the hype: how a
model turns text into tokens and predicts the next one (Day 1), the real differences between
AI, machine learning, and agentic AI (Day 2), what a context window is and isn't (Day 3), the
model landscape and how to compare it (Day 4), what "multimodal" genuinely means versus
demo-ware (Day 5), and how reasoning models trade time for accuracy (Day 6). Week 2 moved from
understanding to building: how an API call, a system prompt, and temperature turn a chatbot
into a product (Day 8), how RAG and embeddings ground a model in your own data (Days 9–10),
when to reach for prompting versus RAG versus fine-tuning (Day 11), and how tool use, MCP, and
agents let a model actually take actions in the world (Days 12–14).

Week 3 was about what it takes to make any of that survive contact with production: measuring
whether a system actually works with evals (Day 15), managing hallucinations with grounding

## The exercise: a real one-page AI adoption plan

You're going to write a genuine one-page AI adoption plan for your own business domain — your
team, your function, your company, whatever scope is real for you. Not a hypothetical
company, not a template answer copied from a slide deck. Yours.

The plan has four parts. Keep the whole thing to one page — that constraint is deliberate.
A plan that needs five pages to say what it means hasn't actually decided anything yet; one
page forces you to prioritize and cut, which is the same discipline Day 22 asked of picking
use cases, now applied to the whole document.

### 1. Vision (2–3 sentences)

Why is AI actually worth pursuing here — for your specific team or business, not "AI" in the
abstract? Is the case cost, customer experience, a new capability you couldn't offer before,
or a competitive necessity you can't ignore? Pick the real reason, not the impressive-sounding
one. A vision specific enough to matter is also specific enough to rule some things out —
if your vision could paste unchanged into any other company's plan, it's too generic to guide
a real decision.

### 2. Use-Case Portfolio (a short list)

List 2–4 real candidate use cases from your actual work. For each one, apply Day 22's test:
is it genuinely **high-volume** and **well-defined**, or does it just sound exciting? Then add
a rough **build vs. buy vs. partner** instinct for each (Day 23) — you don't need a final
answer, just your honest first read. A portfolio that's all ambition and no filtering isn't a
portfolio; it's a wish list.

### 3. Operating Model (a few lines)

Who would actually own this, in your real organization? Even a rough first-draft answer is
worth writing down:

- Who approves what gets built or bought?
- Who owns getting the data ready (Day 24)?
- Who owns watching the cost and the system once it's live (Days 18–19)?

If your honest answer to any of these is "no one, currently," write that down too. That gap
is exactly the kind of thing this exercise is supposed to surface.

### 4. Risk Posture (a few lines)

What's your stance on reliability and guardrails (Day 16), security (Day 17), and governance
and human-in-the-loop review (Day 25) — for the specific use cases you just listed, not AI in
general? A customer-facing use case with real financial stakes needs a very different posture
than an internal drafting tool nobody ships without review. Match the caution to the actual
stakes; don't default to either extreme.

Write the whole thing now, in your own words, about your own domain. Real effort, one page.

## Pressure-test it with an AI assistant

Now do something a little different: use AI to find the holes in your AI plan. Paste your
one-page draft into an AI assistant — the same kind of tool you tested back on Day 7 — and
ask it explicitly to challenge you, not to compliment you. A prompt like "read this plan and
try to find the weakest part of it — don't just tell me it looks good" works better than
asking "what do you think?", which tends to produce polite agreement.

Push on the same questions the AI should be pushing on:

- **Is the vision actually specific enough to say no to something?** Or does it read like it
  could apply to any team in any company?
- **Are the use cases really high-volume and well-defined**, or did something exciting-sounding
  but genuinely risky sneak into the list — the exact trap Day 22 warned about?
- **Does the risk posture actually match the stakes of what you proposed**, or is it a
  paragraph tacked on at the end that doesn't connect to the specific use cases above it?
- **Is the operating model naming real people and real ownership**, or vague roles that don't
  map to anyone who'd actually pick up the work?

Apply the same discipline from Day 16 here: don't just trust a fluent, confident response —
including the AI's critique itself. If it pushes back on something, check whether the pushback
is actually right before you rewrite around it. Then revise your plan based on what genuinely
holds up. That loop — draft, pressure-test, revise — is worth repeating a second time if the
first pass surfaced real problems.

## What you should walk away with

Not a finished, final strategy document — one page written in an hour or two isn't that, and
doesn't need to be. What you should walk away with is a real first draft, built on thirty days
of actually understanding the pieces underneath it, plus a habit worth keeping long after
today: matching the tool to the task, demanding a real eval instead of a confident-sounding
answer, testing before trusting, and planning for the people and the operating model, not just
the technology. Take this plan into an actual conversation at work. Revise it as you learn
more. Day 30 isn't the end of learning about this — it's the start of using it.

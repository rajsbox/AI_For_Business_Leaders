Evals, which you saw earlier this week, tell you whether an AI system worked well on a test
set, before launch. Today is about what happens after that — how you actually know it's
still working well once real people are using it, every single day. That gap between "we
tested it and it passed" and "we know it's working right now" is where a lot of AI projects
quietly go wrong, long after everyone stopped worrying about them.

## The gap a passed eval doesn't cover

Imagine a restaurant that gets inspected once, passes with a great score, and never gets
checked again. The kitchen could still slowly slide into bad habits — nobody would know
until a customer got sick and complained. A one-time eval is that inspection. It's real and
useful, but it's a snapshot from one moment, not an ongoing guarantee.

Once an AI system is live, its inputs keep changing — real customers ask things your test
set never covered, a vendor quietly updates the underlying model, someone edits a prompt to
fix one complaint and accidentally breaks something else. None of that shows up in last
month's eval report. You need a way to keep watching, not just a certificate from opening
day.

## Observability: actually seeing what the system is doing

**Observability** is the ability to see what your AI system is doing in production — what
real users are asking, what answers it's giving, how often it's slow, wrong, or expensive,
and why.

Think of the difference between a dashboard in a car and driving blind. A dashboard shows
you speed, fuel, and warning lights continuously, while you're driving. Driving blind means
you only find out something was wrong when you already hit something. Most AI projects that
work fine in a demo and quietly go bad in production were, in effect, driving blind — nobody
had built a dashboard for what happens after launch.

## Monitoring: watching the key numbers over time

**Monitoring** is continuously tracking a handful of important numbers so problems get
caught quickly, instead of surfacing weeks later as a customer complaint. Typical things
worth watching: is the system taking longer to answer than usual, is the rate of failed or
low-quality answers creeping up, is the cost per request (Day 18) rising unexpectedly.

The value of monitoring isn't the numbers themselves — it's catching a slow, quiet drift
early, while it's still a small fix, instead of discovering it three weeks later when a
customer has already had a bad experience and told other people about it.

## Tracing: investigating one specific interaction

**Monitoring** tells you *something* is wrong in aggregate. **[Tracing](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/concepts/signals/traces/)**
is what lets you find out *why*, for one specific case. It means being able to look back at
one exact interaction and see, step by step, what actually happened — what was in the
context window at the time, what tools the system called (Day 12), and exactly what the
model received and returned.

A useful comparison: monitoring is like a building's smoke alarm going off — it tells you
something's burning somewhere. Tracing is like being able to walk back through exactly what
happened in the kitchen five minutes before the alarm went off. Without tracing, "something
went wrong" is often as far as anyone can get — there's no way to actually dig into a
specific bad answer and explain it to a customer, a regulator, or your own team.

## Versioning: knowing exactly what produced a result

AI systems change over time in ways that are easy to miss: a vendor updates their model
behind the scenes, someone edits the system prompt, a setting like Day 8's temperature gets
tweaked for a different project and never gets set back. **Versioning** means keeping a
record of exactly which model, prompt, and configuration produced a given result.

Think of it like keeping the recipe and the exact ingredient batch alongside every dish a
kitchen serves. If a customer says a dish tasted different this week, "what exactly changed
since last week, and can we reproduce it?" is only answerable if someone wrote down what
went into it each time. Without versioning, a real quality shift and ordinary day-to-day
variation look identical — you can't tell if something actually changed, and you can't
reliably reproduce or debug a specific past result.

## LLMOps: the discipline that ties it together

**[LLMOps](https://ml-ops.org/content/mlops-principles)** is the general practice of
applying observability, monitoring, tracing, and versioning — plus deployment and ongoing
updates — to AI systems specifically. It's the same idea as **DevOps**, the well-established
discipline of running traditional software reliably, adapted for how AI systems actually
behave and fail.

That adaptation matters because AI failures often don't look like traditional software
failures. A crashed website or an error code is obvious and gets noticed immediately. The
"looks right but is subtly wrong" hallucination problem from Day 16 is much harder to catch
— the system doesn't crash, it just confidently gives a wrong answer, and traditional
monitoring built for crashes and error codes won't flag it on its own. LLMOps is, in large
part, about building the extra layer of watching that this particular failure mode requires.

## What this means when you're approving a project

"We tested it and it worked" describes a moment before launch. "We can see what it's doing
right now, and we'll know quickly if something changes" describes an ongoing operational
capability — and that difference is often what separates a project that stays healthy after
launch from one that quietly degrades until a customer complaint forces someone to notice.

When a team brings you an AI project for production approval, the eval results answer "did
it work in testing?" It's just as fair to ask what observability, monitoring, tracing, and
versioning exist after launch — because that's the answer to "will we know if it stops
working?"

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. A team proudly presents strong eval results and asks for sign-off to launch. Ask: *"Once
   this is live, how will we know if it's still performing this well next month?"* — shifts
   the conversation from a one-time test result to an ongoing operational plan.
2. A customer complains that an AI assistant gave a confidently wrong answer last Tuesday.
   Ask: *"Can we trace that exact interaction and see what it actually received and
   returned?"* — reveals whether anyone can actually investigate a specific bad answer, or
   only shrug and say "AI is like that sometimes."
3. In a budget meeting, someone proposes cutting the "monitoring dashboard" line item as a
   nice-to-have. Ask: *"If cost or error rates started drifting upward next week, how would
   we find out — and how fast?"* — tests whether monitoring is treated as optional polish or
   as the thing that catches expensive problems early.
4. A vendor quietly rolls out a model update, and quality on a task seems to have changed.
   Ask: *"Do we have a record of exactly which model and prompt version produced last
   month's results, versus this month's?"* — exposes whether versioning exists at all, and
   whether the quality shift can even be diagnosed rather than just noticed.

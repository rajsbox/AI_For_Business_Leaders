Nothing new to read today. Weeks 1 and 2 gave you the vocabulary and the mental models. Week
3 has been about what happens once an AI tool leaves the demo and has to survive contact with
real use — measuring it, breaking it, securing it, paying for it, watching it, and getting it

## Week 3 in one paragraph

Before you can trust an AI system, you need a way to actually measure whether it works — an
**eval** (Day 15). Even a system that mostly works will confidently say wrong things, so you
need ways to ground it in real facts and put guardrails around it (Day 16). A working system
is also a target — prompt injection and data leakage are real, practical risks, not
theoretical ones (Day 17). Every request has a real cost, and understanding tokens, pricing,
and caching is how you avoid an AI bill that surprises you (Day 18). Once something is live,
you need to actually watch it running — observability and LLMOps — rather than assume it
keeps working just because it worked in testing (Day 19). And most companies that pilot AI
never get it into real production — Day 20 looked at why that gap is so wide.

That's a lot of "how to keep AI honest and under control." Today you'll feel a small version
of all of it, because you're about to build something and immediately try to break it
yourself.

## The exercise: build one small AI tool, on one real task

Unlike Day 7 — where you tested chatbots that already existed — today you build one. The goal
isn't to end up with something impressive. It's to personally go through the mechanics of
turning "a good prompt" into "a saved, reusable tool," so that the phrase "AI-powered
workflow" stops sounding abstract.

### Step 1: pick one real, recurring task

Choose something from your actual job that:

- You or your team does **repeatedly**, not a one-off.
- Follows a **repeatable pattern** — the input varies, but the shape of the task doesn't.
- Currently takes real manual effort, even if it's only a few minutes each time.

Good candidates:

- Drafting a first-pass reply to a common type of email or request (a customer question you
  see weekly, a standard internal ask).
- Summarising a recurring type of document — meeting notes, a weekly report, a set of
  customer comments — into a consistent format.
- Triaging or categorising incoming requests into a few clear buckets (urgent vs. routine,
  which team should handle it, what type of issue it is).

Avoid picking something high-stakes for your first build. You want a task where a wrong
answer is annoying, not something a customer or a regulator would notice.

### Step 2: pick one tool, and understand what kind it is

You have two broad options. Pick whichever one you have access to — the point is the exercise,
not the specific product.

- **A saved-assistant tool** (for example, a custom project in a chatbot like Claude Projects,
  or a Custom GPT). Underneath, this is really just a **system prompt** (Day 8) — written
  once, saved, and reused — plus, in some tools, some reference material you attach. It's
  something you or a teammate kick off manually each time you need it. Good fit if your task
  is "I open this and use it when I need it."
- **A workflow-automation tool** (for example, n8n or Zapier). This can trigger automatically
  off a real event — a new email arriving, a form being submitted — and chain several steps
  together, closer to the tool-use and agent territory from Week 2 (Days 12 and 14), but
  without writing code. Good fit if your task is "this should just happen when X occurs,"
  not something you want to remember to run.

If you're not sure which fits, default to the saved-assistant option — it's the simpler build,
and still teaches the core lesson.

### Step 3: build it

1. **Write clear instructions.** Apply Day 8's lesson directly: tell it exactly what job it's
   doing, what tone to use, what it should never do, and what "done" looks like. Vague
   instructions produce vague output — the same rule that applies to a person you're
   delegating to.
2. **Add real reference material if the tool allows it.** If your task depends on facts
   specific to your company — a policy, a price list, a template — attaching that document
   is a small, hands-on taste of the grounding idea from Day 9 and Day 16: the tool is more
   reliable when it's reading real material, not guessing from general knowledge.
3. **Test it on real examples from your actual work** — an email you actually received, a
   document you actually need summarised. Not made-up examples. You can only judge whether
   this is useful if you feed it the real thing.
4. **Now try to break it.** Feed it a weird edge case on purpose — an email that doesn't fit
   the usual pattern, a document that's oddly formatted, a request that's ambiguous. This is
   a tiny, informal version of Day 15's evals — you, personally, stress-testing your own
   build before you'd trust it with anything real.

## What to notice while you're building

This is the part worth paying attention to, not just the finished tool:

- **Did writing the instructions feel different from asking a one-off question?** A one-off
  prompt (Day 3) is disposable — you write it, use it, and forget it. A saved system prompt
  has to hold up across many different inputs you haven't seen yet. That difference is the
  whole reason context engineering became its own skill.
- **Where did it fail, or give a strange answer?** Was the fix "be more specific in the
  instructions," "give it more real reference material to ground it in" (Day 9), or did the
  task turn out to be a bad fit for a simple no-code tool at all (Day 11's decision
  framework — some tasks genuinely need more than a system prompt can deliver)?
- **Would you trust this running with no one checking its output?** Be honest. Most first
  builds are a good draft generator, not something to run unsupervised — and knowing which
  one you built is exactly the judgment Day 16 asked you to practice.

## What you should walk away with

Not a polished tool — a working one is a nice bonus, but that's not the point. The real
takeaway is that **"AI-powered tool" almost always means a system prompt, maybe some
reference material, wrapped in an interface someone bothered to save.** You just did that
yourself, in an hour or two, with no code. That's worth more than the specific thing you
built: the next time someone in a meeting describes an "AI workflow" like it's a mysterious
black box, you'll know it's very likely something close to what you just made.

The last 27 days were mostly about the technology and the project mechanics: how models
work, how to build and govern and measure AI systems. Today is different. Today is about
the people who have to actually use the thing, or whose jobs it touches — and it's often
the most underestimated part of an AI rollout. A technically excellent project can still
fail completely if the humans around it aren't brought along.

## Reskilling: deliberate, not accidental

**Reskilling** means helping the people you already employ build the skills to work
effectively alongside AI tools. The mistake most leaders make is assuming this happens on
its own — that people will either resist the new tool or quietly figure it out by
themselves. Neither usually happens well without help.

This course is itself a small example of what reskilling actually looks like: a leader
setting aside real time, day after day, to build AI fluency on purpose, instead of hoping
it happens by osmosis. The same approach scales up to a whole team or company. It takes
dedicated time, real training, and explicit permission to be a beginner at something new —
not just a company-wide email announcing "we're using AI now" and leaving people to sort it
out.

> **Why this matters to you:** an email is not a training program. If the only investment a
> company makes in AI adoption is announcing the tool exists, most people will either avoid
> it or use it badly, and the leader will conclude "AI didn't work here" when the real
> problem was never technical.

## AI-augmented roles: the job changes shape, it doesn't vanish

Many jobs don't disappear because of AI. They change shape — AI takes on part of the work,
and the person focuses on the parts that still need judgment, a relationship, or oversight.
This connects directly to the **human-in-the-loop** idea from Day 25: an AI-augmented role
is often, quite literally, the human standing in that loop.

Two generic, plausible examples of what this looks like in practice:

- A **customer support role**, where AI drafts a first response to a routine question, and
  the person reviews it, personalizes it, and takes over entirely when something escalates.
- An **analyst role**, where AI pulls together an initial summary of the data, and the
  person interprets what it actually means and makes the recommendation that gets acted on.

**The trap to avoid:** describing this honestly is a leadership responsibility, not a
communications problem to be smoothed over. That means not pretending nothing will change
for the people in these roles — it will — and also not overselling automation that isn't
actually happening, just to sound impressive in a town hall. Both distortions get found out
quickly by the people doing the actual work, and both cost trust.

## Why change management matters more here than for ordinary software

Rolling out a new AI tool is not like rolling out a new CRM or a new expense system. AI can
trigger real, reasonable anxiety about job security in a way an ordinary software update
almost never does — and that anxiety is not just an emotional side effect, it's a practical
threat to the rollout itself.

Think of it like introducing a new kitchen appliance to a restaurant staff. If the cooks
believe the appliance is there to replace them, they won't learn to use it well, they'll
find ways to work around it, or they'll quietly sabotage it — none of which shows up as a
clean, visible technical failure. It just shows up later as "the AI project didn't deliver
the results we expected," and nobody can point to exactly why.

Ignoring this human reaction doesn't make it go away. It just makes it invisible until
adoption quietly fails — which is a much harder problem to diagnose and fix than an obvious
bug.

## Org design for AI-native companies: a genuinely open question

Looking further ahead, some companies are starting to rethink not just individual roles but
how entire teams and reporting lines are organized around AI-augmented work. Two directions
worth watching, without assuming either is inevitable for your company:

- Smaller teams doing more, because AI absorbs certain kinds of routine work that used to
  require a larger headcount.
- New roles emerging specifically to manage and improve AI systems day to day — partly the
  same territory as the **observability and LLMOps** work covered on Day 19, now becoming
  someone's actual job title rather than a side task.

This is genuinely exploratory territory, not a settled playbook. Nobody has a proven
blueprint yet for "the AI-native org chart." A leader's job right now is to watch this
thoughtfully and stay curious about it — not to assume their company must radically
restructure immediately just because the idea is circulating.

## Bringing it together

Treat the people side of an AI rollout with the same deliberate planning that Weeks 2 and 3
applied to the technical side — architecture, governance, evaluation, cost. In practice,
that means three things: communicate honestly about what will and won't change, invest real
time in reskilling rather than a one-off announcement, and design roles that pair what AI is
actually good at with what people are still genuinely needed for. Skip any one of these, and
the human reaction — not the technology — becomes the reason the project underperforms.

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. Leadership is about to send a company-wide announcement that "we're rolling out AI
   starting Monday." Ask: *"What's the actual training plan behind this — dedicated time,
   real sessions — or is the announcement the whole plan?"* — exposes whether reskilling is
   a genuine investment or just a memo.
2. A team lead proudly reports that a role has been "fully automated" by AI. Ask: *"Which
   specific parts moved to AI, and which parts is a person still doing — and have we told
   that person clearly?"* — tests whether the change is being described honestly instead of
   oversold.
3. In a rollout planning meeting, someone assumes the team will "just pick it up" once the
   tool goes live. Ask: *"What are we doing to address the reasonable anxiety people will
   have about what this means for their job?"* — surfaces the human-reaction risk before it
   quietly sabotages adoption.
4. A consultant's deck proposes restructuring the team around a new "AI-native org model."
   Ask: *"Is this based on a proven pattern, or are we the experiment here?"* — keeps a
   genuinely unsettled idea from being adopted as if it were a safe, standard best practice.

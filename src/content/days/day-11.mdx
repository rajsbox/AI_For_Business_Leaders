Over the last few days you've picked up three separate ideas: giving the model good
instructions, RAG, and how models search by meaning. Today those pieces come together into
one practical question every leader eventually has to answer: when someone proposes an AI
project, which of the three actual techniques should it use? Get this wrong and you either
overpay for something simple, or underbuild something that needed more.

## Three ways to change what a model does

There are only three real ways to make an AI model behave the way you want. They differ in
how much work it takes to set up, and how easy it is to change your mind later.

**Prompting** means simply telling the model what you need, in the message itself, every
single time. This includes the upgraded version you saw on Day 3 — context engineering,
where you don't just ask a question but hand the model the right supporting information
alongside it, like a current price list or a customer's account details. It's the lightest
touch: no setup beyond writing good instructions, and you can change it instantly by just
asking differently next time. The catch is that nothing sticks — you (or your software) have
to re-supply the instructions and the information on every single request, and everything
you supply has to fit inside the model's [context window](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_language_model#Context_window),
which you met on Day 1.

**RAG**, which you saw this week, automatically fetches relevant information from your own
documents or database right before the model answers — so it always has current, specific
information without a person manually pasting it in each time. It takes more work up front
than plain prompting, because someone has to build that retrieval system. But it scales to
answering questions over far more information than could ever fit in one context window, and
it stays current automatically as your underlying documents change — update the source
document, and the next answer reflects it.

**Fine-tuning** is different in kind from the other two: it means actually continuing the
training process on your own examples, so the desired behaviour gets baked into the model's
[parameters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_network_(machine_learning)#Parameters)
themselves, rather than being told each time. It's the heaviest touch — the most expensive,
the slowest to set up, and the slowest to change, since updating it means running the training
process again. In exchange, it can teach a model a very specific style, format, or specialised
skill in a way that repeating instructions in a prompt often can't fully achieve.

A simple way to hold all three in mind: prompting is like leaving a sticky note for someone
every morning. RAG is like giving them a key to a filing cabinet they can check whenever they
need a fact. Fine-tuning is like sending them back to school to actually learn a new skill —
worthwhile for something they'll need forever, wasteful for something you'll only ask once.

## The decision framework

Here's the practical test. Ask these three questions, in order, about whatever the model
needs to do:

**Does the model just need to know a fact or follow an instruction for this one
interaction?** Use prompting. Don't build anything more elaborate than you need — a sticky
note doesn't need a filing cabinet.

**Does the model need to answer using a large amount of your own information that keeps
changing?** Use RAG. This is the right tool the moment the information is too big to paste
in every time, or changes often enough that hardcoding it would go stale.

**Does the model need to consistently behave in a very specific way — a format, a tone, a
specialised skill — across everything it does, and is that need stable, not likely to change
often?** Only then consider fine-tuning. It's the right call when you've tried prompting and
it isn't reliable enough, and the behaviour you need is settled, not still evolving.

**The practical norm worth remembering:** most real business AI tools run on prompting and
RAG together, and reach for fine-tuning rarely. Fine-tuning is the most expensive and slowest
option to change, so teams only reach for it once they're confident the need is real,
specific, and stable — not as a first move.

## A common mistake: reaching for fine-tuning too early

A frequent pattern in AI projects: a team hits a limitation with prompting, and someone
suggests "let's just fine-tune it," assuming that's the serious, professional-grade solution.
Often the actual problem is one of the other two — the prompt wasn't specific enough, or the
model needed information it simply didn't have access to, which RAG would solve at a fraction
of the cost and time. Fine-tuning should usually be the last option tried, not the first,
because it's the hardest one to undo if it turns out to be the wrong call.

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. A vendor recommends fine-tuning a model for your customer support tool before anyone has
   tried improving the prompts. Ask: *"Have we tried giving the model better instructions and
   the right documents first, before committing to a fine-tuning project?"* — checks whether
   the heaviest, slowest option is actually necessary, or just the one that sounds most
   impressive to propose.
2. In a budget meeting, a fine-tuning project is pitched as a one-time cost. Ask: *"If our
   product or policies change next quarter, do we have to retrain the model again to keep it
   accurate?"* — surfaces the ongoing cost of keeping a fine-tuned model current, which a
   one-time price tag can hide.
3. During a build review, an engineer says the assistant "just has the company policies in
   its prompt." Ask: *"As those policies grow, will we keep pasting the whole document in
   every time, or should this move to a retrieval system instead?"* — flags the moment a
   project has outgrown plain prompting and needs RAG.
4. In a rollout planning meeting, someone wants the assistant to always respond in a very
   specific, consistent format across thousands of daily uses. Ask: *"Is this a case where
   fine-tuning would actually be more reliable than repeating formatting instructions in every
   prompt?"* — the rare case for genuinely considering fine-tuning, so the question is asked
   deliberately instead of by default.

  {
    term: "Prompting",
    definition: "Giving a model instructions and information directly in the message sent to it. It requires no setup beyond writing the message, but nothing supplied this way is retained — it must be resupplied on each request, within the limits of the model's context window."
  },
  {
    term: "Context engineering",
    definition: "The practice of deliberately assembling the most relevant instructions and supporting information into a prompt, rather than relying on the model's own training knowledge. An upgraded, more deliberate form of prompting."
  },
  {
    term: "RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)",
    definition: "A technique where a system automatically retrieves relevant information from an external source, such as a document store, and supplies it to the model before it answers — keeping answers current without manual copy-pasting."
  },
  {
    term: "Fine-tuning",
    definition: "Continuing a model's training process on a specific set of examples so a desired behaviour, style, or skill becomes built into the model's parameters, rather than being supplied at request time."

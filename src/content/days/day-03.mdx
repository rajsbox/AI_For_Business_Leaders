A year or two ago, the hot skill was "prompt engineering" — writing the cleverest possible
question. That's no longer the main lever. Today you'll see why the real skill shifted to
deciding *what the model gets to see* in the first place, and why that shift changes how you
should be evaluating AI projects at work.

## A quick reminder: what a context window is

As you saw on Day 1, a **[context window](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_language_model#Context_window)**
is how much text the model can "hold in mind" during one conversation — like a notepad you
hand it right before asking your question. It's fixed in size, it resets between separate
conversations, and everything the model uses to answer you has to fit inside it.

That notepad is the subject of today's lesson, because it turns out *what you write on the
notepad* matters far more than *how cleverly you phrase the question at the bottom of it*.

## What a prompt actually is

A **prompt** is simply the instruction or question you give the AI — the message you type
in. "Summarise this contract," "write a follow-up email to this customer," "what's wrong
with this spreadsheet formula" are all prompts.

Early on, people noticed that *how* you phrased a prompt changed the quality of the answer.
Asking "explain this simply, like I'm new to the topic" got a different result than just
pasting a question with no instructions at all. That observation is where "prompt
engineering" came from.

## Why "prompt engineering" stopped being the whole story

**Prompt engineering** means carefully wording a single instruction — choosing the right
phrasing, tone, and structure to get a better answer out of the model. Think of it like
learning to phrase a request to a waiter well: "could I get the sauce on the side, and is
the fish gluten-free?" gets you a better outcome than a vague "just bring me something
good." For a while, that phrasing skill genuinely was most of the game.

But as people built real AI-powered tools — customer support assistants, document
reviewers, coding helpers — they ran into a bigger problem than wording. The model wasn't
failing because the question was worded badly. It was failing because it simply didn't have
the right information in front of it: the current policy document, the customer's order
history, an example of the format the team actually wants.

That's the shift. **Context engineering** is the deliberate practice of curating
*everything* the model sees before it answers — not just the wording of the question, but
which documents get included, which prior messages are kept, which examples are shown, and
what instructions are given about how to behave. The question itself is now just one
ingredient among several, not the whole recipe.

Picture a new employee's first day. Prompt engineering is coaching them on how to phrase a
question well when they ask for help. Context engineering is deciding what's actually in
their onboarding folder — the org chart, the current price list, last quarter's report, a
couple of example emails written the way the company likes — before they're asked to do
anything at all. A perfectly-phrased question from someone with an empty folder still
produces a worse result than an ordinary question from someone with the right folder.

> **Why this matters to you:** when someone on your team says an AI project is "just a
> prompting problem," that's usually the wrong diagnosis today. The more common real problem
> is a context problem — the model isn't being shown the right documents, examples, or
> history. Fixing that means deciding what data and content get fed in, which is a business
> and data-governance decision, not a wording tweak.

## The cost and quality tradeoff of stuffing the window

It's tempting to think "more context is always better" — just paste in everything that
might be relevant. It isn't that simple, for two reasons.

**Cost.** As you saw on Day 1, everything in the context window is made of tokens, and
tokens are what you're billed for. A longer context window means a bigger bill on every
single request, not a one-time cost.

**Quality.** Beyond a certain point, adding more content can make answers *worse*, not
better. If you paste in ten documents to answer a question that only needed one, the model
has to find the relevant needle in a much bigger haystack, and it's more likely to blend in
irrelevant details or miss the point buried in the middle. A cluttered desk doesn't help you
find the one file you need faster — it makes it slower, and it's the same for a model.

**The trap to avoid:** treating "give the model more" as a safe default. The better question
is always "what's the smallest, most relevant set of information this answer actually
needs?" — the same discipline you'd apply to briefing a busy colleague, not a "just attach
everything" instinct.

## What this means for how you evaluate AI work

Once you accept that context, not clever wording, is the real lever, a few things follow for
how you lead AI projects:

- When a team says an AI feature "isn't working well," the fix is rarely "have someone write
  better prompts." Ask what the model is actually being shown when it answers, and whether
  that's the right material.
- Decisions about which documents, data sources, and examples feed an AI tool are business
  decisions — about accuracy, currency, and relevance — not just an engineering detail. They
  deserve the same scrutiny you'd give to what goes into a briefing pack for a new hire.
  We'll go deeper on the specific technique for doing this well — retrieval — on Day 9.
- Budget conversations about AI tools should account for context size, not just "which
  model." A tool that stuffs a huge amount of material into every request will cost more per
  use than one that's selective, and won't necessarily answer better.

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. A team reports their AI assistant is giving vague or generic answers, and someone
   suggests "we just need a better prompt." Ask: *"Or is it not being shown the right
   documents in the first place? What's actually in its context right now?"* — redirects a
   wording fix toward the more likely real cause.
2. A vendor's pricing model charges per request, and someone on your team wants to paste an
   entire policy manual into every query "just to be safe." Ask: *"What's this costing us
   per request if we send the whole manual every time, versus just the relevant section?"*
   — surfaces a cost problem before it shows up on next month's invoice.
3. In a project review, an engineer says they've "engineered a great prompt" for a new
   feature. Ask: *"What information does it actually have access to when it answers — and
   who's responsible for keeping that current?"* — moves the conversation from wording to
   the ongoing, more important question of data ownership.
4. During a rollout planning meeting, someone proposes feeding the model every historical
   document "so it has full context." Ask: *"Could adding all of that actually make answers
   worse by burying the relevant part, not better?"* — challenges the "more is always
   safer" instinct before it becomes an expensive default.

You'll increasingly hear three terms in AI news and vendor pitches: sovereign AI, edge AI,
and AI factories. They all sound like they're about the same thing — where AI computing
happens. They're not interchangeable, and none of them require you to do anything today.
Today is about knowing what each one actually means, and — more usefully — knowing when, if
ever, it's relevant to a decision you're making.

## Sovereign AI: who controls the infrastructure

**Sovereign AI** is the idea that a country — sometimes a large company or an entire
regulated industry — wants AI infrastructure and capability located within its own borders
and under its own control, rather than depending entirely on AI services run by companies
based in other countries.

A simple comparison: think of why a country cares about having some domestic energy
production or domestic food supply, even when perfectly good imported alternatives exist.
It's not that imports are bad. It's that depending entirely on another country or company
for something that's becoming critical to your economy and security is itself a risk worth
managing — a supply chain risk, not a quality judgment.

Day 4 covered frontier versus open-weight models — that's about who controls the *model*.
Sovereign AI is a different question: who controls and where does the underlying
*infrastructure* run — the data centers, the chips, the servers actually doing the
computing. You can use an open-weight model and still run it entirely on infrastructure
owned by a foreign company, and you can use a closed frontier model hosted on
infrastructure guaranteed to sit within your own country. The model question and the
infrastructure question are separate, and a business can face either one independently of
the other.

> **Why this matters to you:** this mostly shows up as government policy — incentives, or
> sometimes requirements — pushing certain organizations toward AI infrastructure or
> vendors that offer sovereignty guarantees. If your business is a government contractor,
> or operates in a heavily regulated industry like healthcare, finance, or defense, this is
> worth tracking. For most other businesses, sovereign AI is a background trend to be aware
> of, not something that changes a vendor decision this year.

## Edge AI: processing on the device, not in the cloud

**[Edge AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_computing)** means running AI processing
directly on a local device — a phone, a factory sensor, a piece of equipment — instead of
sending everything to a distant cloud server and waiting for a response.

A simple comparison: it's the difference between doing a quick calculation in your head
right now, versus calling a colleague, waiting for them to work it out, and waiting for
them to call you back with the answer. The phone call gets you a more careful answer from
someone with more resources at their desk. Doing it in your head is instant, and it still
works if the phone line is down — but you're limited to what you can actually work out
yourself.

That trade-off is exactly the one edge AI makes. It's faster, because there's no round trip
to a distant server and back. It keeps working even without a reliable internet connection.
But it's usually less powerful than what a large cloud server can run, because the device
itself has far less computing power than a data center.

Edge AI matters for two kinds of situations. First, anything needing an instant response —
a safety system on a factory floor that can't wait for a network round trip, or a device
that has to keep functioning without constant connectivity. Second, situations where sending
data to the cloud raises privacy or security concerns of its own — Day 17 covered how easily
sensitive data can leak out through everyday AI use, and keeping processing local is one way
some organizations manage that risk.

**The trap to avoid:** for most everyday business AI use — drafting emails, summarizing a
document, answering a question — cloud-based AI remains the simpler default, and there's no
reason to complicate it. Edge AI is a deliberate choice for a specific latency- or
connectivity-sensitive need, not a general upgrade every business should be moving toward.

## AI factories: purpose-built infrastructure for AI at scale

**AI factories** is a newer term for large-scale, purpose-built facilities designed
specifically to run massive AI computing workloads — essentially specialized industrial
infrastructure for AI, rather than a general-purpose data center repurposed for the job.

A simple comparison: a car factory is built differently from a general warehouse, because
it's designed around one specific, intensive job rather than flexible general storage. An
AI factory is the same idea applied to computing — infrastructure purpose-built around the
specific, intensive demands of training and running large AI models, rather than general
computing.

For most business leaders, this is infrastructure happening upstream, built and operated by
large infrastructure and cloud providers. You almost certainly don't need to build one
yourself, and there's no action item here for most businesses. But the term is worth
knowing, because a major AI vendor's access to — or partnership with — this kind of
large-scale infrastructure affects their capacity and reliability to serve customers at
scale. That's a fair, specific thing to ask a major AI vendor about, rather than a fair
thing to worry about building yourself.

## Knowing when each one actually matters

None of these three trends require action from most business leaders directly. The useful
skill isn't memorizing the definitions — it's knowing when, if ever, one of them is relevant
to a decision actually in front of you.

- Is your business in a regulated or government-adjacent industry? Then sovereign AI is
  worth tracking.
- Does a specific use case need an instant, offline-capable response, or does it involve
  data too sensitive to send off-site? Then edge AI is worth evaluating for that use case
  specifically.
- Are you evaluating a major AI vendor's capacity and reliability at scale? Then their
  access to AI-factory-level infrastructure is a fair question to raise in that
  conversation.

For most everyday business AI use, none of these three change what you'd actually do. But
recognizing the terms means you won't be caught out when they come up in a meeting — and
you'll know which one, if any, is actually relevant to your situation, instead of treating
all three as equally urgent.

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. A government-contract renewal is coming up, and someone mentions "data residency
   requirements" for the first time. Ask: *"Is this about where our AI vendor's model runs,
   or specifically about where the infrastructure and data physically sit?"* — surfaces
   whether this is a sovereign AI requirement before it becomes a surprise late in
   procurement.
2. A product team proposes adding an AI feature to a device that's used in areas with
   patchy internet access. Ask: *"Does this need to work instantly and offline, or can it
   tolerate a short delay while it calls out to the cloud?"* — decides whether edge AI is
   actually the right call here, instead of defaulting to cloud out of habit.
3. In a vendor evaluation meeting, a sales rep emphasizes how much AI computing capacity
   their company controls. Ask: *"What does that capacity actually guarantee us in terms of
   reliability if demand spikes across your customer base?"* — turns a vague infrastructure
   flex into a concrete answer about service reliability.
4. During a budget review, someone suggests investing in edge AI hardware "to stay ahead of
   the trend." Ask: *"Which specific use case of ours actually needs local, offline
   processing — or are we buying this because it's a trend rather than a fit?"* — keeps
   spending tied to an actual business need rather than a headline.

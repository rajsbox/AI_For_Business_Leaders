You'll hear "AI," "machine learning," "deep learning," "generative AI," and "agentic AI"
used as if they're interchangeable. They're not. They're more like nesting dolls, with one
new capability layered on top at the end. Today you'll learn exactly how they fit together,
so you can tell when a vendor is using the fanciest term available instead of the accurate
one.

## The nesting dolls: AI, Machine Learning, Deep Learning, GenAI

Think of these four terms as boxes inside boxes, each one a smaller, more specific piece of
the one before it.

<JargonMapDiagram />

**[Artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence)** is the
biggest box — any software that does something normally requiring human thinking. That's a
huge range: a chess program from the 1990s counts. A spam filter counts. So does ChatGPT.

**[Machine learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_learning)** is a smaller box
inside AI. Instead of a programmer writing exact rules ("if the email contains this word,
mark it as spam"), the software learns patterns by looking at lots of examples — like
showing someone a thousand photos of cats and a thousand photos of dogs, and letting them
work out the difference themselves rather than describing "cat" and "dog" in words.

**[Deep learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_learning)** is a smaller box inside
machine learning. It's a particular way of doing the learning, using something called a
neural network — loosely inspired by how brain cells connect to each other, arranged in
many stacked layers. "Deep" refers to having many of these layers, not to any special
intelligence. This is the approach behind most of the AI breakthroughs of the last ten
years, including image recognition and voice assistants.

**[Generative AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_artificial_intelligence)**, or
GenAI, is a smaller box inside deep learning. It's deep learning specifically trained to
*create* new things — text, images, audio, video — rather than just recognise or sort
things. ChatGPT, Claude, and Midjourney are all generative AI. This is the box most of the
recent hype is actually about.

> **Why this matters to you:** when a vendor says their product "uses AI," that sentence is
> almost meaningless — a decade-old spam filter also "uses AI." Ask which box they actually
> mean. "We use machine learning" and "we use generative AI" are very different, specific
> claims with very different capabilities and costs.

## Agentic AI: a capability, not a bigger box

**Agentic AI** doesn't fit neatly inside the nesting dolls — it's better thought of as
generative AI plus one extra ability: taking actions, not just producing text.

A generative AI tool writes you an email draft and stops. An agentic AI tool can draft the
email, check your calendar, decide the best time to send it, and send it — carrying out a
multi-step task on its own, checking its own progress along the way, with far less
step-by-step instruction from a person. We'll go deeper on how this actually works on
Day 14.

A simple way to picture the difference: generative AI is like a very capable assistant who
writes anything you ask for, but only when you ask. Agentic AI is like handing that
assistant a goal and a set of tools, and letting them figure out the steps and carry them
out themselves.

**The trap to avoid:** "agentic" has become a popular label because it sounds impressive.
Plenty of products calling themselves "agentic" are really just generative AI with a single
extra button — not something actually planning and carrying out multiple steps on its own.
The test isn't the label; it's whether the system can take a goal, break it into steps, and
act on more than one of those steps without a person approving each one individually.

## Traditional ML isn't obsolete — it's often the better tool

It's easy to assume that if generative AI is the newest thing, it must be the best choice
for everything. It isn't. Plain machine learning — the kind that predicts a number or
sorts something into a category, without generating any text — is often faster, cheaper,
and more accurate for the job it's actually built for.

Think of it like tools in a kitchen. A food processor (generative AI) is flexible and can
do many things reasonably well. But if all you need is to boil an egg, a simple pot (a
traditional ML model) does it faster, cheaper, and more reliably than the food processor
ever will.

Examples of traditional ML doing the job better than GenAI would: predicting which
customers are likely to cancel a subscription, flagging a fraudulent transaction, or
forecasting next month's demand for a product. These are narrow, well-defined, numbers-in,
numbers-out problems — exactly what traditional ML is built for, and exactly the kind of
task where a business shouldn't reach for the newest, most expensive tool by default.

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. A vendor's pitch deck says their product "is powered by AI," and nobody in the room
   asks what that actually means. Ask: *"When you say AI, do you mean generative AI, or a
   simpler predictive model? Those are very different things to evaluate."* — forces a
   vague claim into a specific, checkable one.
2. A colleague describes a new internal tool as "basically an AI agent." Ask: *"Can it
   actually carry out a multi-step task on its own, or does it generate one response and
   stop?"* — separates a genuinely agentic system from generative AI wearing a trendier
   label.
3. In a project scoping meeting, someone suggests using a large language model to predict
   which customers might churn next month. Ask: *"Wouldn't a simpler prediction model do
   this faster and cheaper, since we're really just sorting customers into categories?"* —
   stops an expensive tool from being used on a job a cheaper one already does well.
4. During a roadmap review, a team proposes "an agentic AI system" for a task that's really
   just answering questions from a document. Ask: *"Does this actually need to take
   independent actions, or is it really a generative AI lookup task with an impressive
   name?"* — keeps the project scoped to what it actually needs, not what sounds best in a
   slide.

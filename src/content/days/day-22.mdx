Weeks 1 through 3 were about how AI works, how it gets built into a real product, and how
you keep it reliable once it's live. Starting today, Week 4 shifts altitude. The question
stops being "how does this work?" and becomes "where in my business should I actually point
it?" That's a strategy question, not a technical one — and it's the one that decides whether
your first AI project becomes a success story or a quiet write-off.

## Most AI projects don't fail on the technology

When an AI pilot fizzles out, the postmortem rarely says "the model wasn't good enough."
More often, the real story is that the project was aimed at the wrong problem from day
one — something too rare to matter, too fuzzy to build reliably, or too dependent on
judgment nobody could actually write down. The tool might have worked fine. It just never
had a fair problem to solve.

Today is about avoiding that mistake, with a simple test you can apply before a single
line of a project plan gets written: is this task high-volume, and is it well-defined?
Get both right, and you've found a strong first candidate. Get either wrong, and you're
setting up an expensive lesson.

## High volume: the task has to happen often

A workflow is high-volume when it happens many times a day or week — not a handful of
times a year. This matters for two very practical reasons.

First, the math only works at volume. A tool that saves two minutes on a task done twice a
year saves almost nothing — you'll spend more time building and maintaining it than it will
ever save. The same two minutes, saved on a task done five hundred times a week, adds up to
real hours very fast.

Second, and just as important: high volume means you get real usage data quickly. Think of
it like a restaurant testing a new dish. If it only sells the dish twice a year, it takes
years to learn whether customers actually like it. If it sells the dish two hundred times a
week, it knows within days. AI is the same — a high-volume task gives you the real-world
examples you need to build the **evals** you learned about on Day 15, the tests that
actually tell you whether the thing is working, not just whether it looked good in a demo.

## Well-defined: the task has to have a describable pattern

The second property is just as important, and it's the one teams skip past most often. A
task is well-defined when someone experienced could write down, in fairly concrete terms,
roughly how they decide or do it — even if it still takes some judgment. It doesn't have to
be a rigid, step-by-step recipe. It just has to be describable.

The opposite is a task that's highly ambiguous: deeply dependent on unwritten institutional
knowledge, or one where two experienced people would genuinely disagree on the right answer.
Think of the difference between a recipe and a family cook who "just knows" when a dish is
ready by smell and feel alone. The recipe can be handed to someone else and followed. The
"just knows" skill can't be written down at all — and if you can't write it down for a
person, you can't hand it to an AI model either.

Well-defined tasks are easier to build reliably — this is exactly why clear system prompts
(Day 8) and grounding a model in the right documents through
[RAG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrieval-augmented_generation) (Day 9) work so much
better when the task itself has a clear shape. They're also easier to eval, because you can
actually describe what a good answer looks like — which is the whole foundation an eval is
built on.

> **Why this matters to you:** volume and clarity aren't nice-to-haves — they're the two
> inputs everything else in this course depends on. No volume means no data. No clear
> definition means no way to know if an answer is right. Skip either one, and every later
> step — evals, guardrails, monitoring — is built on sand.

## The trap: chasing the most exciting-sounding use case

The most common mistake isn't picking a bad idea. It's picking the *best-sounding* idea —
the one that would make the flashiest demo, or the one a competitor just announced in a
press release — instead of the one that actually fits the two criteria above.

An impressive-sounding use case that's low-volume or poorly-defined is far more likely to
turn into an expensive failed pilot — the exact gap between pilot and production covered on
Day 20 — than a boring, high-volume, well-defined one ever is. Nobody writes
a press release about "we automated a routine data-entry step that ran 400 times a day." But
that boring project is the one far more likely to actually ship, actually get used, and
actually save money.

## Where to actually look for good candidates

Good candidates are usually hiding in plain sight, buried in tasks nobody thinks to mention
because they're "just part of the job." Three places to look:

- **Anything someone describes as "I do this every single day."** That's a volume signal
  said out loud, without anyone needing to run a study to find it.
- **Any task that already has a template, checklist, or standard set of steps written
  down somewhere.** A written checklist is a strong sign the task is already well-defined —
  someone has already done the work of describing the pattern for you.
- **Any task that's currently a bottleneck because only one or two people know how to do
  it well.** That's often a sign of a well-defined skill that simply hasn't been written
  down yet — and writing it down to brief an AI system can be valuable on its own, even
  before any AI gets built.

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. Someone in a strategy meeting proposes an AI project because a competitor just
   announced something similar. Ask: *"Is this actually a high-volume task for us, or are
   we chasing a headline?"* — separates a real opportunity from a reaction to someone
   else's press release.
2. A team pitches an AI tool for a task they describe as "more of an art than a science."
   Ask: *"Could an experienced person on the team actually write down how they make that
   call?"* — tests whether the task is well-defined enough to build and eval, before any
   money is spent.
3. In a project shortlist review, two candidate use cases are on the table: one sounds
   impressive but happens rarely, the other is mundane but happens constantly. Ask: *"Which
   one actually gives us enough real examples to know if it's working within a few weeks?"*
   — pushes the decision toward volume and away from novelty.
4. A budget conversation is about to greenlight a project built around a task only one
   senior person can currently do well. Ask: *"Do we actually have a clear, written pattern
   for this, or are we hoping the AI will somehow absorb knowledge that's only in one
   person's head?"* — catches a poorly-defined task before it becomes a stalled pilot.

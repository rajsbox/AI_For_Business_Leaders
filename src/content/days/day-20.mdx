Today's title borrows a pattern you've probably seen written up in different forms, with
different exact numbers, in surveys and industry reports over the last couple of years: a
large share of companies say they've "adopted" AI in some form, but a much smaller share
have it running as an actual production system. We're not going to treat "79%" and "11%" as
verified numbers to defend — the precise figures move around depending on who's counting and
what they mean by "adopted." What's genuinely well-documented, and worth your time today, is
the *gap itself*. Lots of individual people using an AI chatbot is common. A reliable AI
system quietly doing real work inside a real business process, day after day, without
someone watching it like a hawk, is rare. Today is about why that gap exists, using
everything you learned this week as the answer.

## Two very different things called "using AI"

**Informal adoption** looks like this: someone on your team pastes a report into a chatbot to
summarise it. Someone else drafts emails with it. It's genuinely useful, it spread on its
own, and nobody had to approve a budget for it. This is real, and it's not nothing — but it's
a person choosing, each time, whether to trust the output before they hit send.

**Production** is different in kind, not just in scale. It means an AI system is wired into
an actual business process — answering real customers, approving or flagging real
transactions, drafting documents that go out under your company's name — running on its own,
continuously, without a person checking every single output before it counts.

Think of the difference between a person occasionally using a calculator, and a calculator
being wired into your company's payroll system so it runs unattended every two weeks. Both
are "using a calculator." Only one of them needs to be right every single time, with nobody
watching.

## Why a successful pilot isn't evidence of a production-ready system

This is the one idea to take from today: **a pilot going well is not the same evidence as a
production system being ready.** A pilot succeeding tells you the AI can do the task under
the conditions the pilot happened to test. It tells you almost nothing about whether it can
do the task reliably, safely, and affordably at real volume, on real customers, including the
messy and unusual cases nobody thought to try.

Walk through why, and you're really just walking back through this week:

**No real evals, just a good-looking demo.** A pilot is usually judged on a handful of
examples someone picked to show it off — often the easy ones, sometimes without even
meaning to cherry-pick. That's not the same as [evals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaluation)
run against a large, representative, and deliberately difficult set of real cases. A model can
look impressive on ten hand-picked examples and still fail regularly on the ten-thousandth
real customer message. Without real evals, "the demo went well" is the only evidence anyone
has — and it's weak evidence.

**Hallucinations and missing guardrails don't show up in a friendly demo.** In a pilot,
people mostly ask reasonable questions and read the answers charitably. Real usage is
messier: ambiguous questions, edge cases, people testing the limits on purpose, and answers
nobody double-checks because the system is now "just running." Without grounding and
guardrails proportional to the risk, a system that never once made up an answer during the
pilot can start doing so regularly once it's facing real, unpredictable volume.

**Security wasn't considered because the pilot never felt like a "real" system.** A pilot
often gets waved past the security review that a traditional software project would go
through, because it's "just a chatbot" or "just a trial." But once it's live, it's handling
real data and real inputs from real people — including people who might deliberately try to
manipulate it. Skipping that thinking during the pilot doesn't make the risk go away; it just
delays when someone notices it.

**The pilot's cost was fine — at pilot volume.** Ten people testing a tool for a week costs
very little. Ten thousand customers using it every day is a completely different bill.
Without modelling cost at realistic production volume, a pilot that "barely cost anything" can
turn into a production system with a monthly bill nobody budgeted for.

**Nobody built a way to know if it's working once it's live.** A pilot has a small, attentive
audience watching it closely and reporting anything odd. A production system has neither —
which is exactly why it needs its own eyes on it. Without monitoring and tracing in place,
the first sign that something is wrong is often a customer complaint, or a much bigger
problem, weeks after it started.

None of these five gaps are exotic. Each one is a specific, previously-covered topic that a
successful demo simply never had the chance to expose.

## What successful deployments actually share

The companies that do get AI systems reliably into production aren't the ones with access to
a better model. They're the ones who treat the jump from pilot to production as its own real
project, with its own requirements — not as "the demo worked, so let's turn it on for
everyone."

In practice, that means before scaling a pilot up, they can point to:

- **Real evals**, run against a large, realistic, and deliberately hard set of test cases —
  not just a handful of clean examples that happened to work in the room.
- **Guardrails sized to the task's actual risk.** A tool that drafts internal meeting notes
  needs far less scaffolding than one that tells a customer their refund was approved.
- **A security review that assumes this is a real system**, because by the time it's serving
  customers, it is one — regardless of how casually it started.
- **A cost model built at real production volume**, not pilot volume, so nobody is surprised
  by the first full month's bill.
- **Monitoring and tracing switched on before the first real customer sees it**, not added
  after the first complaint arrives.

None of this is exotic engineering. It's closer to ordinary project discipline — the kind any
serious software rollout would go through — applied to AI instead of being skipped because AI
still feels new and experimental.

## The one question that replaces "did the pilot go well?"

When someone proposes moving a successful pilot into full production, "did the pilot go
well?" is the wrong question to lead with — it's already been answered, and the answer was
never going to be "no." The useful question is really five questions in one, and you now have
all five from this week:

1. Has this been eval-tested at something closer to real, realistic scale — not just the
   examples from the demo?
2. Does it have guardrails proportional to what happens if it gets something wrong?
3. Has security actually been considered, on purpose, not skipped because it "felt casual"?
4. Do we know the real cost at real volume, not pilot volume?
5. Will we actually know if something breaks once it's live — or will we find out from a
   customer?

A "yes" to all five is what separates a system ready for production from a pilot that simply
hasn't failed publicly yet.

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. A team proudly reports that their AI pilot "went really well" and wants approval to roll
   it out to every customer next month. Ask: *"Well by what measure — a handful of examples
   we liked, or a real eval set that includes the hard and unusual cases?"* — separates a
   genuinely tested system from one that simply hasn't been tested hard yet.
2. In a budget meeting, someone presents the pilot's AI spend as the expected ongoing cost.
   Ask: *"That's the cost at pilot volume — what does it look like at the volume we'd
   actually run once this is live for everyone?"* — surfaces the real bill before it becomes
   a surprise.
3. During a rollout planning meeting, the security team hasn't been looped in because "it's
   just a chatbot." Ask: *"Once this is answering real customers, isn't it a real system now
   — has security actually looked at it?"* — closes the gap where pilots quietly skip a review
   a comparable piece of software wouldn't.
4. Right before a pilot goes live company-wide, someone asks what happens if it starts
   answering badly. Ask: *"How will we actually find out — do we have monitoring in place, or
   are we relying on customers to tell us?"* — exposes whether anyone will notice a problem
   before it's already caused damage.

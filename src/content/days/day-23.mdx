Once you've found the right use case (yesterday's topic), the next real decision isn't
"should we use AI here?" — it's "where do we actually get this from?" That's a sourcing
decision, and it has real tradeoffs most teams rush past because everyone wants to get to
"yes" too quickly.

## The four ways to get an AI solution

There are roughly four paths to getting AI working on a business problem, and they sit on a
spectrum from fastest-and-generic to slowest-and-exact.

<SourcingSpectrumDiagram />

**Buy a general platform.** This means using an existing, general-purpose AI product — a
chatbot subscription, or an AI "copilot" feature already built into software you use, like
an assistant bolted onto an office suite or a CRM. It's the fastest way to get started, with
the lowest upfront cost and effort. The tradeoff: it's built for everyone in general, not
your specific workflow, so it may not fit your exact process — and you're limited to
whatever the vendor decided to build.

**Buy a vertical vendor.** A vertical AI vendor builds AI for one industry or one specific
job — say, a tool built just for reviewing legal contracts, or just for a particular kind of
customer support in a particular industry. This is more tailored to your actual workflow
than a general platform, and usually faster to get real value from than building it
yourself. The tradeoff: you're dependent on that vendor's roadmap and pricing, and for a
very niche need, a vendor built specifically for it may not exist yet.

**Build custom.** Your own team builds a custom AI system using the pieces covered across
this whole course — prompting, [retrieval-augmented generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrieval-augmented_generation)
(Day 9), tool use (Day 12), maybe a full agent (Day 14) — tailored exactly to your specific
process and data. This gives you full control and the best possible fit to your exact
workflow. The tradeoff: it's the most expensive and slowest option, and it requires real
in-house capability — an engineering team who actually understands the concepts from Weeks
1–3 — to build it. Remember Day 19's lesson that a shipped system still needs ongoing
operational care after launch: a custom build has to handle all of that itself, forever,
with no vendor to call.

**Partner.** This is a middle path worth naming separately: working with an implementation
partner or consultancy to build something semi-custom. It gets you more tailoring than a
general platform, without needing to grow a full in-house AI engineering team from scratch.
The tradeoff: it still costs real money and real time, and you're dependent on the partner's
quality and availability once the project is live. It's a genuine option between fully
buying and fully building — worth knowing exists, rather than assuming the choice is only
"buy" or "build."

> **Why this matters to you:** every one of these paths can be the right answer, depending
> on the use case. The mistake isn't picking any particular one of them — it's picking one
> out of habit or fashion, without weighing the actual tradeoffs against what this specific
> workflow needs.

## A framework for choosing

This is the same idea as Day 11's prompting-vs-RAG-vs-fine-tuning framework, applied to a
bigger decision: not "how do we build the AI," but "where do we get it from." Three
questions do most of the work.

**How unique is this workflow to your business?** A generic task — drafting routine emails,
summarizing meetings — is well served by a general platform. A somewhat specific task fits a
vertical vendor or a partner. A workflow that's deeply unique to exactly how you operate,
with no real equivalent elsewhere, is the strongest case for building.

**How much in-house AI capability do you actually have, or are willing to build?** If your
team has little AI capability today and no plan to grow it, buying or partnering is almost
always the sounder choice — building without the team to maintain it just delays the pain.
If you already have, or are genuinely investing in, a capable team, building becomes far
more viable.

**How fast do you actually need this?** If the honest answer is "we need something running
now," buy. If you can invest the time for a better long-term fit, build or partner become
realistic options.

## The honest caution: build isn't the "serious" choice

It's tempting to treat "build" as the sophisticated, serious option and "buy" as the lazy
shortcut. That's backwards. Plenty of well-run companies buy for things that aren't their
core differentiator, and build only for the small number of things that genuinely are. A
company that insists on building everything in-house isn't being rigorous — it's spending
scarce engineering time on problems someone else has already solved well.

This is the same "match the tool to the task" discipline from earlier in the course — Day
11's prompting-vs-RAG-vs-fine-tuning choice, Day 20's caution about pilots that never reach
production — just applied to sourcing strategy instead of technical technique. The right
question is never "which option sounds most impressive to say in a board meeting?" It's
"which option actually fits this specific workflow, this team, and this timeline?"

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. A vendor is pitching their general AI copilot as the answer to a very specific,
   specialized internal process. Ask: *"Is this built for workflows like ours specifically,
   or is it a general tool we'd be bending our process around?"* — surfaces whether you're
   buying a fit or buying convenience.
2. In an internal build review, an engineering team is excited to build a custom system for
   a fairly routine, generic task. Ask: *"Is this workflow actually unique to us, or could a
   platform we already pay for do most of this today?"* — checks whether "build" is solving
   a real need or just satisfying the team's appetite to build something.
3. In a budget conversation, someone frames a vertical AI vendor's subscription as
   "expensive compared to just building it ourselves." Ask: *"Once we count the engineering
   time to build it and the ongoing cost to maintain it after launch, is that really
   cheaper than the subscription?"* — pulls the comparison out of sticker price and into the
   full cost, including the upkeep Day 19 warned about.
4. In a rollout planning meeting, someone proposes bringing in an implementation partner.
   Ask: *"What exactly do we own once the partner's engagement ends, and who maintains it
   after that?"* — makes sure "partner" isn't quietly becoming "build, without a plan for
   who runs it afterward."

Days 16 and 17 covered guardrails and security — things a good team does because it's good
practice. Today is about a different question: how do you actually know those practices are
being followed, consistently, across every AI project in your business — not just the one
the engineering team happens to be proud of? That's governance. It's the organizational and
policy layer sitting on top of the technical one, and increasingly, parts of it are becoming
legally required rather than just advisable.

## Governance is the layer that makes the technical work count

A team can build excellent guardrails (Day 16) and solid security practices (Day 17) for one
project, and have none of that for the next one, because nothing forced consistency across
the business. Governance is the set of policies, processes, and accountability that closes
that gap — who has to sign off before an AI system launches, what has to be documented, who
reviews high-stakes decisions, and what happens when something goes wrong.

Think of it like food safety in a restaurant chain. One kitchen having a brilliant head chef
who personally checks every dish is not food safety — it's luck. Food safety is the health
code, the inspections, and the paperwork that make sure *every* kitchen in the chain meets
the same standard, whether or not that particular chef happens to be careful. Governance
does the same job for AI: it's what turns "our best team does this well" into "our business
does this reliably."

## The EU AI Act: a real law, worth knowing exists

The **[EU AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai)**
is a genuinely significant piece of regulation — the European Union's law governing how AI
systems can be built and used. As a business leader, you don't need to become a regulatory
expert on it. You need to know three things: that it exists, its general shape, and when to
bring in help.

Its general shape is a **risk-based approach**: AI systems are regulated more or less
strictly depending on how much potential harm they could cause. An AI system that helps
decide something like someone's employment, credit, or legal rights faces much stricter
requirements than a low-stakes internal tool, like one that drafts internal meeting
summaries. The stricter rules generally mean more documentation, more testing, and more
human oversight before and during use.

**This is the point where this course stops, on purpose.** Specific compliance obligations —
what exactly applies to your business, what deadlines matter, what the actual penalties are
— is a legal question for qualified legal counsel, not something a course lesson can
responsibly tell you. Regulations like this are detailed, still evolving, and depend heavily
on your specific business, your specific use case, and where your customers are. Treat
anything that sounds like a specific compliance answer from an AI course, a vendor, or a
LinkedIn post with real suspicion, and loop in legal or compliance expertise early for
anything AI-related that touches the EU or touches a higher-stakes decision about a person.

> **The leader's job here isn't to become a lawyer.** It's to know this exists, know the
> general shape — stricter rules for higher-stakes uses — and know early enough to ask legal
> "does this apply to us?" before a project launches, not after a regulator asks the same
> question.

## Responsible AI: good practice, independent of the law

**Responsible AI** is broader than legal compliance. It's the practice of building and using
AI in a way that's fair, accountable, and doesn't cause unintended harm — regardless of
whether a specific law requires it. A few concrete examples of what this looks like day to
day:

- Checking that an AI system isn't systematically worse for some groups of people than
  others — for example, an AI screening tool that performs noticeably worse for one group of
  applicants than another.
- Being transparent that a customer is talking to an AI, rather than letting them assume
  they're speaking with a person.
- Having a real process for someone to flag or appeal a decision an AI system was involved
  in, instead of a decision that's effectively unchallengeable.

The reason this matters even where no law demands it: unfair or harmful outcomes damage
trust and reputation regardless of their legal status. A business that quietly produces
worse outcomes for some customers, or hides that a bot is a bot, pays for that in trust long
before any regulator gets involved — and that damage doesn't undo itself once the legal
question is settled.

## Audit trails: Day 19's tracing, now for accountability

Day 19 introduced tracing — the ability to look back at one specific interaction and see
what actually happened, mainly so an engineering team could debug it. An **audit trail** is
the same underlying record, kept and used for a different purpose: not "can we debug this
technically," but "can we show a regulator, an auditor, or an affected customer exactly what
happened and why, if asked."

It's the difference between a security camera an IT team occasionally checks to fix a
network issue, and the same footage being treated as an official record that has to be kept,
retrievable, and presentable if there's ever a formal inquiry. Same recording, but once it
has to hold up to outside scrutiny, how it's kept, how long it's retained, and how completely
it captures what happened all become more important. The technical capability from Day 19
now also has to serve this accountability purpose.

## Human-in-the-loop: keeping a person in the approval chain

Day 12 covered tool use — a model's ability to actually take actions, not just produce text.
Left fully on its own, a system with tool use can act entirely autonomously: decide, and do,
with nobody checking in between. **Human-in-the-loop** is a deliberate governance choice to
keep a person reviewing or approving an AI system's output or decision before it takes
effect, especially for higher-stakes decisions.

This connects directly to Day 16's stakes-matching principle: the higher the stakes of a
decision, the stronger the case for keeping a human in the loop rather than letting the
system act fully on its own. A tool that drafts an internal email and sends it automatically
is low stakes enough that full autonomy is often fine. A system that could deny someone a
loan, reject a job applicant, or take an action with legal weight is exactly the kind of case
where a person should review the output before anything happens to a real person because of
it.

## What this looks like when you're the one approving a project

Governance isn't red tape bolted onto AI as an afterthought. It's the accountability layer
that makes everything from Days 15 through 19 — evals, guardrails, security, cost,
observability — actually mean something to someone outside your own engineering team: a
regulator, an auditor, or a customer who was harmed by a bad decision.

A business that can say "here's our eval process, here's our guardrails, here's our audit
trail, here's who reviews our high-stakes decisions" is in a fundamentally stronger position
— legally and reputationally — than one that can only say "it worked in the demo." As a
leader, that's the standard worth asking your teams to meet before you sign off on anything
AI touches a real customer or a real decision about a person.

## Four questions that instantly sound credible in an AI meeting

1. A team is proposing to roll out an AI system that helps screen job applicants across
   several EU markets. Ask: *"Has legal or compliance reviewed this against the EU AI Act's
   risk category for this kind of use, before we build any further?"* — makes sure a
   higher-stakes, EU-facing use case gets the right expertise involved early, instead of
   after launch.
2. In an internal build review, an engineer says the new AI tool "logs everything so we're
   covered." Ask: *"Is that log good enough to hand to an auditor or a regulator if they
   asked, or is it just good enough for us to debug with?"* — separates a technical trace
   built for engineers from a real audit trail built to satisfy outside scrutiny.
3. During a budget conversation, someone argues that a human review step before a high-stakes
   AI decision goes out is slowing the team down and should be automated away. Ask: *"What's
   the actual harm if this decision is wrong, and does that harm justify removing the human
   in the loop?"* — forces the stakes of the decision, not the speed of the workflow, to
   drive the call.
4. In a rollout planning meeting, someone says "we don't need to worry about responsible AI,
   we're not doing anything illegal." Ask: *"Legal is one bar — but could this system still
   produce unfair or damaging outcomes for some customers even if nothing about it breaks the
   law?"* — separates the legal compliance question from the broader, and separately
   important, responsible AI question.
